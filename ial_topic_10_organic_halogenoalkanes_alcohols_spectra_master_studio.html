⚠️ PEF Reminders — Principal Examiner Feedback (2019–2025) from chemistry-wiki wchNN-examiner-insights
  • Curly arrows: must start from a bond or lone pair and end at an atom/ion — not from empty space, not to bonds (every organic mechanism paper).
  • Dehydration of alcohols: reagent is conc. H₂SO₄ (or Al₂O₃), NOT NaOH/ethanol (that's elimination of halogenoalkanes) — only 25% scored this (Jan 2020).
  • Oxidation of primary alcohol: product water (not H₂); to get an aldehyde use DISTILLATION (reflux gives some carboxylic acid) (Jun 2019, Oct 2024).
  • Reactivity: tertiary halogenoalkane faster (tertiary carbocation formed) — students wrongly say the halogenoalkane IS the carbocation; C–Hal bond strength governs halogen trend (weaker C–I → faster) (Oct 2020, Jan 2022, Oct 2024).
  • Water must be present with ethanol solvent for substitution to be favoured over elimination (Jun 2019).
  • IR: “bond stretching” must be stated explicitly; alcohol = O–H stretch (not C–O); aldehyde C–H stretch ≈2720–2820 cm⁻¹ distinguishes aldehyde from ketone (C=O alone insufficient) (Jun 2023, Jan 2020, Jun 2024R).
  • IR O–H vs C=O: O–H (carboxylic acid/alcohol) can't distinguish aldehyde from ketone (Jun 2024R).
  • Nitrile naming: count the C of –CN in the chain (butanenitrile vs propanenitrile) (Oct 2020, Oct 2024, Jun 2024R).
  • Skeletal formula: show H on heteroatoms (–OH not –O) (Jan 2023, Jun 2023).
  • Acidified dichromate is invalid as a generic –OH test (gives orange→green for tertiary alcohols/aldehydes too); PCl₅ or Na are the –OH tests (Jun 2019).

Topic 10 is the chemistry of the C–X and –OH groups — and how to identify every group here by mass & IR spectroscopy. The thread: a polar bond makes a carbon electrophilic, so a nucleophile can attack it.

General principles (10A), halogenoalkanes (10B), alcohols (10C) and spectra (10D). Every code elaborated below.

Spec 10.1 - 10.5General Principles

1. Reaction Types & Mechanisms (10A)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Classify reactions as addition, elimination, substitution, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis or polymerisation.
  • A mechanism is the step-by-step route showing electron movement with curly arrows.
  • Heterolytic bond breaking (both electrons to one atom) gives ions — an electrophile (electron-pair acceptor) and a nucleophile (electron-pair donor).
  • A polar bond (e.g. C–X, C=O) creates a $\delta^+$ carbon that a nucleophile attacks.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Curly arrows start from a lone pair or a bond, never from a positive charge. A nucleophile donates a pair; an electrophile accepts a pair — don't swap them.

Spec 10.6 - 10.7Halogenoalkane Basics

2. Naming & Classifying Halogenoalkanes

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Name halogenoalkanes (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-) and draw structural, displayed and skeletal formulae.
  • Primary (1°): the C–X carbon is attached to one alkyl group.
  • Secondary (2°): attached to two alkyl groups.
  • Tertiary (3°): attached to three alkyl groups.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Count the carbon neighbours of the C–X carbon, not the hydrogens. 2-chloro-2-methylpropane is tertiary (three methyl groups on the C–Cl carbon).

Spec 10.8Halogenoalkane Reactions

3. Five Reactions — the Nucleophile Decides

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • aq. KOH, warm → alcohol (OH⁻ is a nucleophile, substitution).
  • ethanolic KOH, heat → alkene (OH⁻ is a base, elimination).
  • aq. AgNO₃ in ethanol → halide precipitate (water is the nucleophile; used to compare rates).
  • alcoholic NH₃, heat & pressure → amine (NH₃ is the nucleophile).
  • alcoholic KCN, heat → nitrile (CN⁻ is the nucleophile) — lengthens the carbon chain by one.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The solvent is decisive: aqueous KOH gives substitution (alcohol), ethanolic KOH gives elimination (alkene). State the solvent and the role of OH⁻ (nucleophile vs base). KCN adds a carbon; NH₃ gives an amine.

Spec 10.9Nucleophilic Substitution Mechanism

4. Drawing the Mechanism (primary)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Show the nucleophile's lone pair attacking the $\delta^+$ carbon (curly arrow from lone pair to C).
  • Show the C–X bond breaking, both electrons going to the halogen (curly arrow from bond to X).
  • Products: the substituted molecule + a halide ion (X⁻).
  • Apply to aq. KOH (→ alcohol) and NH₃ (→ amine). (SN1/SN2 detail is Unit 4.)
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Two curly arrows, both starting from an electron source. The halogen leaves as X⁻ with the bonding pair. Watch it animate in Tab 3.

Spec 10.10 - 10.13Hydrolysis Rates · CP5

5. Rates of Hydrolysis (CP5)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Compare rates using aq. AgNO₃ in ethanol — time how fast the silver halide precipitate forms.
  • By halogen: C–I fastest > C–Br > C–Cl slowest — explained by bond enthalpy (C–I weakest, breaks most easily).
  • By class: tertiary > secondary > primary for the SN1 contribution.
  • Precipitate colours: AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The halogen trend is about C–X bond strength, not polarity (C–F is most polar but unreactive — bond too strong). Ethanol is the solvent so the organic halide dissolves. Try the rate lab in Tab 3.

Spec 10.14CP6

6. CP6 — Chlorination of 2-methylpropan-2-ol

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • A tertiary alcohol + concentrated HCl → 2-chloro-2-methylpropane (a tertiary halogenoalkane).
  • Tertiary alcohols react with HX directly (no catalyst needed) — the substitution is easy.
  • The product is separated in a separating funnel, washed, dried and distilled.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

This is a substitution of –OH by Cl. Know the purification steps (separating funnel → drying agent → distillation) and why each is done.

Spec 10.15 - 10.16Alcohol Basics

7. Naming & Classifying Alcohols

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Name alcohols (-ol) and draw structural, displayed and skeletal formulae.
  • Primary (1°): the C–OH carbon has one alkyl group (e.g. propan-1-ol).
  • Secondary (2°): two alkyl groups (e.g. propan-2-ol).
  • Tertiary (3°): three alkyl groups (e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The class depends on the carbon bearing the –OH. This classification decides the oxidation product — the single most tested alcohol fact.

Spec 10.17Alcohol Reactions

8. Combustion, Halogenation & Elimination

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Combustion in oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O.
  • PCl₅ → chloroalkane + POCl₃ + HCl (steamy fumes) — a test for the –OH group.
  • 50% H₂SO₄ + KBr → bromoalkane; red P + I₂ → iodoalkane.
  • Concentrated H₃PO₄, heat → alkene + water (elimination / dehydration).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

PCl₅ gives misty/steamy HCl fumes — the positive test for –OH. Mechanisms are NOT required here. H₃PO₄ (not H₂SO₄) is preferred for dehydration to avoid side reactions.

Spec 10.18Alcohol Oxidation · CP7

9. Oxidation with K₂Cr₂O₇ / H⁺ (CP7)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Primary → aldehyde if distilled as it forms (positive Benedict's/Fehling's test).
  • Primary → carboxylic acid if heated under reflux (positive Na₂CO₃/NaHCO₃ test — effervescence).
  • Secondary → ketone (reflux).
  • Tertiary → no reaction (no H on the C–OH carbon).
  • Dichromate goes orange → green when oxidation occurs; write the oxidant as [O].
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Distil to stop at the aldehyde; reflux to go all the way to the acid. Tertiary alcohols stay orange (no reaction). Predict the product in Tab 4.

Spec 10.19Preparation Techniques

10. Preparing & Purifying a Liquid Product

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Heating under reflux — boil without losing volatile liquid (condenser returns vapour).
  • Separating funnel — separate two immiscible liquid layers.
  • Distillation — collect a pure liquid by boiling point.
  • Drying with an anhydrous salt (e.g. MgSO₄) — removes traces of water.
  • Boiling-temperature determination — confirms identity/purity.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Know the order and purpose of each step: reflux/react → separate → wash → dry → distil → check bp. A sharp boiling point = pure.

Spec 10.21Mass Spectrometry

11. Reading a Mass Spectrum

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • The molecular ion peak (M⁺) has the highest m/z = the relative molecular mass.
  • Fragmentation: the molecule breaks into pieces; each peak is a fragment ion.
  • Use the fragments to suggest a structure (e.g. m/z 15 = CH₃⁺, 29 = C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺, 31 = CH₂OH⁺, 43 = CH₃CO⁺).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Read M⁺ for the Mr, then match fragments to lost groups. Practise on the interactive spectrum in Tab 5.

Spec 10.22 - 10.23IR Spectroscopy · CP8

12. Reading an IR Spectrum (CP8)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • O–H: broad ~3200–3600 (alcohol); very broad ~2500–3300 (acid).
  • C=O: strong ~1680–1750 (aldehyde/ketone/acid).
  • C=C ~1620–1680; C–H just below/above 3000; N–H ~3300–3500; C–X ~500–800.
  • Use absorptions to deduce which functional groups are present (CP8: analysing unknowns).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A broad absorption ~3300 = O–H; a sharp one ~1700 = C=O. A carboxylic acid shows BOTH a very broad O–H and a C=O. Explore peaks in Tab 5.

🚧 Scope boundary

SN1/SN2 mechanisms are tested in Unit 4 (WCH13) — here you only need the nucleophilic-substitution mechanism for primary halogenoalkanes. Alkene chemistry (electrophilic addition) is Topic 5; carbonyl/carboxylic-acid reactions in depth are Topic 15.

General Principles · Spec 10.1–10.5

Know Your Reaction Types & the Electron Arrows

Seven reaction classes, plus the nucleophile/electrophile idea that drives organic mechanisms.

＋

Addition

Two molecules join into one; a double bond opens up. e.g. ethene + HBr → bromoethane.

－

Elimination

A small molecule (HX or H₂O) is removed; a double bond forms. e.g. bromoethane + ethanolic KOH → ethene.

⇄

Substitution

One atom/group swaps for another. e.g. bromoethane + aq. KOH → ethanol (Br replaced by OH).

↑O

Oxidation

Gain of O / loss of H. e.g. ethanol → ethanal (with [O] from dichromate).

↓O

Reduction

Loss of O / gain of H. e.g. aldehyde → primary alcohol.

💧

Hydrolysis

Breaking a bond with water. e.g. halogenoalkane + water → alcohol + HX.

⛓

Polymerisation

Many monomers join into a long chain. e.g. ethene → poly(ethene).

🎯 Nucleophiles & electrophiles
Nucleophileelectron-pair donor — has a lone pair. e.g. OH⁻, NH₃, CN⁻, H₂O.
Electrophileelectron-pair acceptor — electron-deficient. e.g. the δ⁺ carbon in C–X.

Heterolytic fission (both bonding electrons go to one atom) makes these ions. A polar C–X bond leaves the carbon δ⁺ — that's the electrophilic site a nucleophile attacks.

🎯 Classify these (click to reveal)
CH₃CH₂Br + KOH(aq) → CH₃CH₂OH + KBr — substitution (hydrolysis)
CH₃CH₂Br + KOH(ethanolic) → CH₂=CH₂ + KBr + H₂O — elimination
CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O — oxidation
CH₂=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂Br — addition

Engine A · Spec 10.6–10.14

Halogenoalkanes — the Nucleophile Decides

5 concept cards
🎬 The nucleophilic substitution mechanism (SN2, primary)

Example: 1-bromopropane + OH⁻ → propan-1-ol. The nucleophile's lone pair attacks the δ⁺ carbon as the C–Br bond breaks and Br⁻ leaves — both curly arrows start from an electron source.

1. Aqueous KOH → alcohol (10.8i)
CH₃CH₂Br + KOH(aq) → CH₃CH₂OH + KBr  ·  warm, aqueous

OH⁻ acts as a nucleophile, donating a lone pair to the δ⁺ carbon; Br⁻ leaves. This is nucleophilic substitution (also a hydrolysis).

Learning goals
  • State reagent (aq. KOH/NaOH), conditions (warm) and product (alcohol).
  • Name the mechanism and the role of OH⁻.
Examiner tips
  • Must say aqueous — ethanolic gives elimination instead.
  • OH⁻ is a nucleophile here, not a base.
2. Ethanolic KOH → alkene (10.8ii)
CH₃CH₂Br + KOH(ethanolic) → CH₂=CH₂ + KBr + H₂O  ·  heat

OH⁻ acts as a base, removing an H from an adjacent carbon; the C–Br bond breaks and a C=C forms. This is elimination.

Learning goals
  • State reagent (ethanolic KOH), conditions (heat) and product (alkene).
  • Explain OH⁻'s role as a base removing H⁺.
Examiner tips
  • Ethanolic is the keyword for elimination.
  • Unsymmetrical halides can give two alkene products (H removed from either side).
3. Alcoholic ammonia → amine (10.8iv)
CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br  ·  ethanolic NH₃, heat & pressure

NH₃ acts as a nucleophile (lone pair on N) attacking the δ⁺ carbon, giving an amine. Excess NH₃ limits further substitution.

Learning goals
  • State reagent (alcoholic/excess NH₃), conditions (heat, pressure) and product (amine).
  • Identify NH₃ as the nucleophile.
Examiner tips
  • Use excess NH₃ to favour the primary amine (otherwise the amine reacts again).
  • The product is an amine, not an amide.
4. Alcoholic KCN → nitrile (10.8v)
CH₃CH₂Br + KCN → CH₃CH₂CN + KBr  ·  ethanolic KCN, heat

CN⁻ acts as a nucleophile. The product has one more carbon than the halogenoalkane — this is how you lengthen the carbon chain.

Learning goals
  • State reagent (alcoholic KCN), conditions (heat) and product (nitrile).
  • Explain why this lengthens the chain (the CN carbon is added).
Examiner tips
  • Count the carbons: bromoethane (2C) → propanenitrile (3C).
  • Attack is through the carbon of CN⁻.
5. AgNO₃/ethanol — comparing hydrolysis rates (10.10–10.13, CP5)
R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻  then  Ag⁺ + X⁻ → AgX(s)

Water (the nucleophile) hydrolyses the halide; the released X⁻ gives a silver halide precipitate. Time to precipitate = rate.

Learning goals
  • Halogen trend: C–I > C–Br > C–Cl (weaker C–X bond breaks faster).
  • Class trend: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • Colours: AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow.
Examiner tips
  • The trend follows bond enthalpy, not polarity. Ethanol dissolves the organic halide. Try the lab below.

Aqueous KOH → alcohol (substitution). The solvent decides everything.

❌ Trap: "C–F is most reactive (most polar)"✅ Truth: C–I is most reactive (weakest bond)

❌ Trap: KCN keeps the same chain length✅ Truth: it adds one carbon

The CN carbon joins the chain — the key chain-lengthening reaction.

❌ Trap: curly arrow from the δ⁺ carbon✅ Truth: arrows start from electrons

From the nucleophile's lone pair to C; from the C–X bond to X.

Engine B · Spec 10.15–10.20

Alcohols — Class Decides the Product

4 concept cards
1. Combustion & the –OH test (10.17i, ii)
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O (combustion)
ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl↑ (steamy fumes)

Alcohols burn completely in air. PCl₅ replaces –OH with Cl and releases misty HCl fumes — the qualitative test for the –OH group.

Learning goals
  • Write the combustion equation.
  • Describe the PCl₅ test and its positive result (steamy fumes).
Examiner tips
  • The fumes are HCl — test with damp blue litmus (turns red) or NH₃ (white smoke).
2. Halogenation & dehydration (10.17ii, iii)
ROH + HBr → RBr + H₂O (50% H₂SO₄ + KBr)
ROH → alkene + H₂O (conc. H₃PO₄, heat)

Make bromoalkanes from KBr + 50% H₂SO₄ (generates HBr in situ); iodoalkanes from red P + I₂. Concentrated H₃PO₄ dehydrates an alcohol to an alkene (elimination).

Learning goals
  • State the reagents for making bromo- and iodoalkanes from alcohols.
  • State the dehydration conditions (conc. H₃PO₄, heat) and product (alkene).
Examiner tips
  • H₃PO₄ is preferred over H₂SO₄ for dehydration (fewer side reactions). Mechanisms not required.
3. Oxidation with acidified dichromate (10.18, CP7)
1° + [O] → aldehyde (distil) · 1° + 2[O] → acid (reflux)
2° + [O] → ketone (reflux) · 3° → no reaction

Oxidant: K₂Cr₂O₇ / dilute H₂SO₄, written as [O]. Colour: orange → green when oxidation happens. Aldehydes give a positive Benedict's/Fehling's test; acids fizz with Na₂CO₃/NaHCO₃.

Learning goals
  • Match each alcohol class to its product(s).
  • Distil for aldehyde, reflux for acid; tertiary = no reaction.
  • State the colour change and the confirmation tests.
Examiner tips
  • Distillation vs reflux is the classic exam fork. Predict the product in Module 2.

The standard sequence: react (reflux) → separate layers (separating funnel) → wash → dry (anhydrous salt) → distil → measure boiling point.

⚡ Alcohol trap pairs
❌ Trap: reflux gives the aldehyde✅ Truth: reflux gives the acid; distil gives the aldehyde

Reflux keeps oxidising the aldehyde to the acid. Distil removes it before that.

❌ Trap: tertiary alcohols oxidise to ketones✅ Truth: tertiary alcohols don't oxidise

No H on the C–OH carbon ⇒ no reaction ⇒ stays orange.

❌ Trap: the colour change is green → orange✅ Truth: orange → green

Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange) is reduced to Cr³⁺ (green).

❌ Trap: dehydration uses H₂SO₄✅ Truth: concentrated H₃PO₄

Phosphoric acid avoids oxidation/side reactions.

❌ Trap: PCl₅ tests for C=C✅ Truth: PCl₅ tests for –OH

Misty HCl fumes confirm the alcohol group.

❌ Trap: rinse the separating-funnel product with water last✅ Truth: dry after washing, then distil

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Halogenoalkanes, Alcohols & Spectra

SN1 vs SN2

Primary: SN2 (one step, backside attack). Tertiary: SN1 (carbocation intermediate). Reagents/conditions differ.

Dehydration vs elimination

Alcohol → alkene is conc. H₂SO₄ (or Al₂O₃). NaOH/ethanol is elimination of HALOGENOALKANES — only 25% scored this.

IR bond identification

O–H stretch (broad, ~3200-3600), not C–O. Students identify the wrong bond.

Alcohol oxidation

Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇: orange → green. Primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; secondary → ketone.

PCl₅ test

Misty fumes of HCl = positive test for –OH.

Displayed formulae

Draw the full molecule first, then identify broken bonds — helps correct connectivity.

Dehydration of Alcohols vs Elimination of Haloalkanes (Jan 2020 PEF)Using $\text{NaOH/KOH}$ in ethanol to dehydrate an alcohol (only 25% scored this mark!)Alcohol $\rightarrow$ alkene (dehydration) requires concentrated $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ (or conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) and heat (or passing vapour over heated $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$). $\text{KOH}$ in hot ethanol is ONLY for eliminating $\text{HX}$ from a halogenoalkane!
Hydrolysis Rate of 1° vs 3° & C–Cl vs C–I (Oct 2020, Jan 2022, Oct 2024)Writing "2-chloro-2-methylpropane is a more stable tertiary carbocation" or "C–Cl is less polar"3° halogenoalkanes hydrolyse faster because they react via a more stable tertiary carbocation INTERMEDIATE (positive inductive effect of 3 alkyl groups). For C–Cl vs C–I, the C–I bond has a lower bond enthalpy (+228 vs +346 kJ mol⁻¹) so it breaks more easily.
Nitrile Naming & Chain Lengthening (Oct 2020, Jun 2024R, Oct 2024)Naming $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CN}$ (from bromoethane + KCN) as "ethanenitrile" or "cyanopropane"Always count the carbon atom of the $-\text{CN}$ group in the longest chain: bromoethane (2C) + KCN $\rightarrow$ propanenitrile (3C); 1-bromopropane (3C) + KCN $\rightarrow$ butanenitrile (4C).
IR Spectroscopy & Aldehyde vs Ketone (Jan 2020, Jun 2023, Jun 2024R)Omitting the word "stretch", attributing ~3300 cm⁻¹ to C–O, or claiming C=O distinguishes aldehyde from ketoneAlways state bond stretching and wavenumber range: broad O–H stretch at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ (alcohol) or 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ (acid); strong C=O stretch at 1680–1750 cm⁻¹; aldehyde is distinguished from ketone by the aldehyde C–H stretch at ~2720–2820 cm⁻¹ (or Fehling's test).
Mass Spec Fragment Ions & Skeletal Heteroatom H (Jan 2023, Jun 2023)Writing neutral radicals ($\text{CH}_3\text{CO}$) for $m/z$ peaks, or drawing skeletal alcohol as $–\text{O}$ instead of $–\text{OH}$Every species detected in MS must carry a positive charge (e.g. $m/z\ 31 = [\text{CH}_2\text{OH}]^+$, $m/z\ 43 = [\text{CH}_3\text{CO}]^+$ or $[\text{C}_3\text{H}_7]^+$). In skeletal formulae, always write the H on heteroatoms ($\text{–OH}$, $\text{–NH}_2$).
