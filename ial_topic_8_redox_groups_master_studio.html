The cast of Topic 8
Group 1 Group 2 Group 7

Group 1 loses 1 electron (→ +1), Group 2 loses 2 (→ +2), Group 7 gains 1 (→ −1). Every reaction in this topic is an electron transfer — that is the redox thread.

Redox language (8A), the reactive metals of Groups 1 & 2 (8B), and the halogens of Group 7 (8C). Every code elaborated below.

Spec 8.1 - 8.4 Oxidation Numbers

1. Oxidation Numbers — the Book-Keeping System

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Oxidation number = the charge an atom would have if all bonds were ionic.
  • Rules: element = 0; monatomic ion = its charge; O = −2 (peroxide −1); H = +1 (metal hydride −1); Group 1 = +1; Group 2 = +2; sum = overall charge.
  • Calculate for any atom: S in $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ = +6; N in $\text{NO}_3^-$ = +5; Mn in $\text{MnO}_4^-$ = +7.
  • Show oxidation number with a Roman numeral: iron(III) = $\text{Fe}^{3+}$; write formulae from oxidation numbers.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Peroxides (O = −1) and metal hydrides (H = −1) are the set-piece exceptions examiners love. Write the sign before the number (+6, not 6+). Roman numerals go in the name, not the formula.

Spec 8.5 - 8.9 Redox & Disproportionation

2. Oxidation, Reduction & Disproportionation

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain. Oxidation number rises on oxidation, falls on reduction.
  • Oxidising agent gains electrons and is itself reduced; reducing agent loses electrons and is itself oxidised.
  • Disproportionation: one element in a single species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced (e.g. Cl₂ in water: 0 → −1 and 0 → +1).
  • Metals form positive ions (loss of e⁻, oxidation number ↑); non-metals form negative ions (gain of e⁻, oxidation number ↓).
  • Use oxidation-number changes to classify a reaction as redox or not.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"The oxidising agent is oxidised" is the classic reversal — it is reduced. For disproportionation you must show the same element going both up and down. Neutralisation and precipitation are usually not redox.

Spec 8.10 Half-Equations

3. Ionic Half-Equations

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Write reduction and oxidation half-equations separately, balancing atoms then charge with electrons.
  • Example: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-$ (reduction); $2\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$ (oxidation).
  • Combine by equalising electrons: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{I}^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^- + \text{I}_2$.
  • Spectator ions are omitted from the full ionic equation.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Electrons must cancel exactly when you combine half-equations. State symbols are expected. Diatomic molecules (Cl₂, I₂) must keep their subscript.

Spec 8.11 - 8.12 Group 1 & 2 Reactivity

4. Reactivity Trends Down Groups 1 & 2

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Reactivity increases down both groups: Li < Na < K; Mg < Ca < Sr < Ba.
  • Reason: more shells ⇒ larger atomic radius ⇒ outer electron(s) further from the nucleus and more shielded ⇒ lower ionisation energy ⇒ easier to lose electrons.
  • Group 1 loses 1 e⁻ (very reactive, stored under oil); Group 2 loses 2 e⁻ (less reactive than Group 1 in the same period).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Always link reactivity to ionisation energy and ease of losing the outer electron(s) — "bigger atoms" alone is not enough. Potassium is more reactive than sodium because its outer electron is easier to remove.

Spec 8.13 - 8.14 Group 1 & 2 Reactions

5. Reactions with O₂, Cl₂, Water & Acids

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • With water: $2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2$; $\text{Ca} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2$. Mg is slow with cold water, fast with steam ($\text{Mg} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{MgO} + \text{H}_2$).
  • With oxygen: $4\text{Na} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ (and $\text{Na}_2\text{O}_2$); $2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{MgO}$ (bright white flame).
  • With chlorine: $2\text{Na} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}$; $\text{Mg} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2$.
  • Oxides + water → hydroxides (alkaline); oxides & hydroxides + dilute acid → salt + water.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Mg + steam gives the oxide (MgO), not the hydroxide — a frequent mix-up. All these metal reactions are redox: the metal is oxidised (0 → +1/+2).

Spec 8.15 - 8.16 Solubility & Thermal Stability

6. Group 2 Solubility & Thermal Stability Trends

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Hydroxide solubility increases down Group 2; sulfate solubility decreases.
  • Thermal stability of nitrates & carbonates increases down the group.
  • Reason (stability): larger cation ⇒ lower charge density ⇒ less polarisation of the nitrate/carbonate anion ⇒ N–O / C–O bonds stay strong ⇒ more energy needed to decompose.
  • Learn the memory systems in Tab 5 — don't rote-learn these trends.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The explanation must use charge density and polarisation — "bigger ions are more stable" alone scores little. Mg²⁺ is small with high charge density ⇒ most polarising ⇒ least stable.

Spec 8.17 - 8.18 Flame Colours & Decomposition Tests

7. Flame Colours & Gas Tests

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Flame colours: Li red, Na yellow, K lilac, Ca orange-red, Sr red, Ba pale green.
  • Cause: heat excites electrons; on relaxation they emit light of a characteristic wavelength — different energy-level gaps per cation.
  • Decomposition gases: CO₂ turns limewater milky; O₂ relights a glowing splint; NO₂ is a brown, acidic gas.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Ca is orange-red and Sr is red — don't swap them. The flame-colour explanation needs "electrons fall back and release energy as light". Try the live flame in Tab 3.

Spec 8.19 Ion Tests

8. Identifying Ions (CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Carbonate / hydrogencarbonate: add dilute acid ⇒ effervescence; gas turns limewater milky. $\text{CO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$.
  • Sulfate: acidify with dilute HCl, add $\text{BaCl}_2$ ⇒ white ppt of $\text{BaSO}_4$.
  • Ammonium: add NaOH and warm ⇒ $\text{NH}_3$ turns damp red litmus blue (and gives white fumes with HCl).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

For sulfate you must acidify first (removes carbonate that would also give a white ppt with Ba²⁺). Full observation = "white precipitate", not just "white". See the labelled bench in Tab 3.

Spec 8.20 - 8.23 Titration & CP3 / CP4

9. Volumetric Analysis & the Two Core Practicals

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Calculate concentration in mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³ from titration data using the mole ratio.
  • Indicators: methyl orange (red in acid / yellow in alkali) and phenolphthalein (colourless in acid / pink in alkali).
  • CP3: find the concentration of HCl. CP4: prepare a standard solution from a solid acid, use it to find [NaOH].
  • Minimise uncertainty: rinse burette/pipette with the solution they will hold, read the meniscus at eye level, repeat for concordant titres (±0.10 cm³), swirl, white tile.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Rinsing the conical flask with the solution it will hold adds extra moles — only rinse with water. Record burette readings to ±0.05 cm³ (two decimal places). Practise the full technique in the 3D lab (Tab 4).

Spec 8.24 - 8.25 Group 7 Trends & Displacement

10. Halogen Trends & Displacement Reactions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Down Group 7: melting/boiling points increase (Cl₂ gas, Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid); electronegativity and reactivity decrease.
  • Colours: Cl₂ pale green, Br₂ red-brown, I₂ grey solid / purple vapour; in cyclohexane: Cl₂ pale green, Br₂ orange/red-brown, I₂ purple.
  • A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{I}^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^- + \text{I}_2$.
  • Oxidising ability decreases down the group: Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Displacement is redox: the halogen is reduced (gains e⁻), the halide is oxidised. Iodine in water is yellow/brown but purple in cyclohexane — examiners ask for the organic-layer colour.

Spec 8.26 Disproportionation of Chlorine

11. Chlorine with Water & Alkali

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • With water: $\text{Cl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{HCl} + \text{HClO}$ — used in water treatment (HClO kills bacteria).
  • With cold dilute NaOH: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{NaClO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (bleach, chlorate(I)).
  • With hot concentrated NaOH: $3\text{Cl}_2 + 6\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 5\text{NaCl} + \text{NaClO}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (chlorate(V)).
  • In each, Cl goes 0 → −1 (reduced) and 0 → +1 or +5 (oxidised) — disproportionation.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Cold dilute gives chlorate(I) (NaClO); hot concentrated gives chlorate(V) (NaClO₃). State the oxidation numbers of Cl in each product to prove disproportionation.

Spec 8.27 - 8.28 Halides + H₂SO₄ & Halide Tests

12. Halides with conc. H₂SO₄ & Silver Nitrate Tests

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Reducing power of halides increases down the group: Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ (Tab 6 has the full memory system).
  • Silver nitrate test (acidify with dilute HNO₃ first): AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow; solubility in NH₃: AgCl dissolves in dilute, AgBr only in concentrated, AgI insoluble.
  • Hydrogen halides + NH₃(g) ⇒ white smoke of ammonium halide; HX + water ⇒ acidic solution.
  • Predict F and At from trends (8.28): F₂ most reactive, At least; HF the weakest reducing halide.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Use nitric acid (not HCl/H₂SO₄, which would add Cl⁻/SO₄²⁻ and give false precipitates). "Cream" for AgBr is the mark-scheme word. Master the H₂SO₄ cluster with the ladder in Tab 6.

Group 7 here is limited to Cl, Br, I (8C) — fluorine and astatine appear only as predictions (8.28). Transition-metal chemistry and electrode potentials are Topics 16/19 (WCH14). Halogenoalkane chemistry is Topic 10.

Reaction Types · Spec 8.5–8.8

Know Exactly What Kind of Reaction You're Looking At

Six types recur across Topic 8. Each card gives the definition, how to spot it, and a worked example with oxidation numbers.

⚡

Redox

Definition: oxidation numbers change — oxidation (loss of e⁻, number ↑) and reduction (gain of e⁻, number ↓) happen together.

Spot it: assign oxidation numbers; if any change, it's redox.

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
Mg: 0→+2 (oxidised) · O: 0→−2 (reduced)
⇌

Disproportionation

Definition: the same element in one species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.

Spot it: one element's oxidation number goes both up AND down.

Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO
Cl: 0→−1 (reduced) and 0→+1 (oxidised)
🔁

Displacement

Definition: a more reactive element pushes a less reactive element out of its compound. A redox reaction underneath.

Spot it: element + compound → new element + new compound.

Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂
Cl₂ reduced (0→−1); I⁻ oxidised (−1→0)
🔥

Thermal Decomposition

Definition: one compound breaks into two or more substances on heating.

Spot it: single reactant + heat → multiple products. Often redox (N changes in nitrates).

2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂
N: +5→+4 (reduced) · O: −2→0 (oxidised)
🧴

Acid–Base (Neutralisation)

Definition: proton (H⁺) transfer from acid to base → salt + water. Not redox — no oxidation numbers change.

Spot it: acid + base/oxide/carbonate; oxidation numbers all unchanged.

MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O
Mg +2, O −2, H +1, Cl −1 throughout
⬇️

Precipitation

Definition: two aqueous solutions react to form an insoluble solid (precipitate). Usually not redox.

Spot it: (aq) + (aq) → (s). Ions swap partners.

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
the sulfate test — white precipitate

Station F Unknown Inorganic Salt Detective & WCH13 Sequential Test Order (Spec 8.17–8.19, 8.27ii)
Why Order Matters: CO₃²⁻ → SO₄²⁻ → X⁻
🧭 Why must you test in the order CO₃²⁻ → SO₄²⁻ → X⁻ on a single unknown solution?
  1. Step 1 — Add dilute HNO₃: Tests for $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (effervescence of $\text{CO}_2$) AND destroys any carbonate so it cannot form white $\text{BaCO}_3(\text{s})$ or $\text{Ag}_2\text{CO}_3(\text{s})$ later. (Never use HCl or $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$!)
  2. Step 2 — Add Ba(NO₃)₂(aq): Tests for $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (white ppt of $\text{BaSO}_4$) AND precipitates out any sulfate (if present, filter it off) so $\text{Ag}_2\text{SO}_4$ cannot interfere. (Never use $\text{BaCl}_2$ if testing the same tube for halides next!)

Remember Group 2 Trends by Understanding Them

Three trends students always mix up. Instead of rote facts, here are two stories and a mnemonic — once you see why, you can't forget.

Hydroxides Hike UP · Sulfates SINK down · Nitrates & Carbonates get TOUGHER down

Now let's see why each one is true, so the words stick.

🪢 Story 1 — The Tug-of-War (solubility, 8.15)

Dissolving is a tug-of-war: Lattice energy holds the solid together, Hydration pulls the ions into water. Both get weaker as the cation grows — but they weaken at different rates depending on the anion's size. Drag the cation slider and switch the anion.

The takeaway: For the small OH⁻, lattice energy falls faster than hydration down the group ⇒ hydration wins more easily ⇒ more soluble down. For the large SO₄²⁻, hydration falls faster ⇒ lattice wins ⇒ less soluble down. Anchor: Mg(OH)₂ = milk of magnesia (barely dissolves) but Ba(OH)₂ dissolves well; MgSO₄ = Epsom salts (dissolves) but BaSO₄ = the "barium meal" (so insoluble it's safe to swallow for X-rays!).
😤 Story 2 — The Bully Cation (thermal stability, 8.16)

A small, highly-charged cation is a bully: its high charge density polarises (distorts) the big nitrate/carbonate anion, weakening the N–O / C–O bonds until they snap on gentle heating. A big, calm cation barely distorts the anion — you need a roaring flame. Pick a cation and watch.

The takeaway: Small + highly charged = bully = breaks the anion = decomposes easily. Down Group 2 the cation gets bigger (charge density ↓, polarising power ↓) ⇒ nitrates & carbonates get more thermally stable. MgCO₃ decomposes in a gentle Bunsen flame; BaCO₃ needs a roaring flame. Group 1 (+1 charge) are even gentler — Na₂CO₃ doesn't decompose at Bunsen temperature at all.

📝 The 4-Mark Examiner QWC Template for Thermal Stability (PEF 2019–2024)

Examiner reports warn that saying "bigger atoms are more stable" scores 0/4. Every full-mark explanation of why $\text{BaCO}_3$ (or $\text{Ba(NO}_3)_2$) is more thermally stable than $\text{MgCO}_3$ must hit these 4 sequential points:

  1. Ionic radius & Charge density: Down Group 2, the ionic radius of the $\text{M}^{2+}$ cation increases while the $+2$ charge stays the same, so the charge density decreases (or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has a smaller ionic radius and higher charge density).
  2. Polarising power of CATION: The smaller $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ cation has a greater polarising power than $\text{Ba}^{2+}$. (Always say ions, never atoms!).
  3. Polarisation of ANION: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ polarises (distorts) the electron cloud of the large $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (or $\text{NO}_3^-$) anion to a greater extent.
  4. Bond weakening: This weakens the C–O (or N–O) covalent bond within the anion more strongly, so less heat energy is needed to break it.
The Hard One · Spec 8.27i

Halides + Concentrated H₂SO₄ — a Memory System, Not a Memory Test

This cluster feels impossible because it's taught as a list. It's actually one story: how hard each halide fights back. Learn the story and every equation follows.

🔑 The one idea: reducing power climbs down the group

All halides can do the acid–base step (give off HX gas). But only a halide that can donate an electron to H₂SO₄ reduces it — and that gets easier down the group:

Cl⁻small — outer shell close to the nucleus, holds its extra electron tight. Weak reducing agent.
Br⁻bigger — outer electron further out, easier to lose. Moderate reducing agent.
I⁻big — outer electron far from the nucleus, very easy to lose. Strong reducing agent.

So: Cl⁻ fights 0 times · Br⁻ fights 1 time · I⁻ fights 3 times. That's the whole thing.

🪜 The Sulfur Staircase — where each halide pushes S

Sulfur in H₂SO₄ starts at +6. A reducing halide knocks it down the stairs. The stronger the halide, the further it falls:

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Redox, Groups 1/2/7

Disproportionation balancing

Balance charges on Cl-containing species FIRST, then add water to balance O/H. Cl: +5 → −1 = 6 e⁻.

Electron count in redox

ClO₃⁻ → Cl⁻: +5 → −1 = 6 e⁻ per Cl. Students count 4 or 5 (forget O and charge).

I₂ in hexane

Purple (I₂ in non-polar solvent). Brown = I₂ in KI(aq); grey = solid; yellow = very dilute aq.

Group 2 trends

Reactivity ↑ down group; hydroxide solubility ↑; sulfate solubility ↓. Mixed-up combinations are the trap.

Flame test acid

Only conc. HCl for cleaning the wire — any other acid scores 0.

Cost without explanation

“Expensive” alone scores 0 — must explain WHAT the cost is for.

3 · Group 2 trends + memory hooks (8.15–8.16)click to collapse
Hydroxides Hike UP: solubility increases down the group (Mg(OH)₂ sparingly soluble → Ba(OH)₂ soluble). Tug-of-war: for small OH⁻, lattice falls faster than hydration.
Sulfates SINK down: solubility decreases (MgSO₄ soluble → BaSO₄ insoluble, the barium meal). For large SO₄²⁻, hydration falls faster than lattice.
Nitrates & carbonates get TOUGHER down: thermal stability increases. Big cation ⇒ low charge density ⇒ less polarisation ⇒ anion bonds stay strong. Mg = bully (decomposes easily); Ba = calm (needs roaring flame).
2Mg(NO₃)₂ → 2MgO + 4NO₂ + O₂ · MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂ · 2NaNO₃ → 2NaNO₂ + O₂ (Group 1 nitrate) · Na₂CO₃ → no decomposition at Bunsen temp
4 · Group 7 reactions (8.24–8.26)click to collapse
Displacement (oxidising ability Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂):
Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ · Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂ · Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂
Disproportionation of chlorine:
Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO (water treatment)
Cl₂ + 2NaOH (cold, dil.) → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O (bleach, Cl: −1 & +1)
3Cl₂ + 6NaOH (hot, conc.) → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O (Cl: −1 & +5)
Colours: Cl₂ pale green · Br₂ red-brown · I₂ grey solid / purple vapour. In cyclohexane: Cl₂ pale green, Br₂ orange/red-brown, I₂ purple.
HX + NH₃(g) → NH₄X (white smoke) · HX + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + X⁻ (acidic)
5 · Halides + conc. H₂SO₄ — the full cluster (8.27i)click to collapse
NaCl — fights 0 times (acid–base only):
NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl↑  (steamy fumes)

NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr↑  (steamy)
2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O  (orange/brown fumes, choking SO₂; S +6→+4)
NaI — fights 3 times:
NaI + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HI↑  (steamy)
2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O  (purple vapour; S +6→+4)
6HI + H₂SO₄ → 3I₂ + S + 4H₂O  (yellow solid; S +6→0)
8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O  (rotten-egg; S +6→−2)
Story: reducing power Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ (bigger ion ⇒ outer electron easier to lose). Sulfur staircase: +6 → +4 → 0 → −2.

☐ Disproportionation = same element up AND down
☐ Peroxide O = −1; metal hydride H = −1

☐ Ca flame = orange-red; Sr flame = red

☐ Halide test: use NITRIC acid, not HCl
