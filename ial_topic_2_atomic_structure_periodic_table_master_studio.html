Spec 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4 Atom & particles

Atomic Structure, Z, A & Counting Particles

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals
  • Describe the atom: a tiny central nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in shells / energy levels
  • State relative mass & charge: proton = 1, +1; neutron = 1, 0; electron ≈ 0 ($\tfrac{1}{1836}$), −1
  • Define atomic (proton) number Z = number of protons (defines the element)
  • Define mass number A = protons + neutrons
  • For a neutral atom: electrons = protons = Z
  • For an ion: electrons = Z − charge (subtract for cations, add for anions); protons & neutrons unchanged
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Electron relative mass is ≈ 0, not exactly 0 — say "negligible / approximately zero". For ions, only the electrons change; students wrongly alter the proton count. A 2+ ion has two fewer electrons than protons. Notation $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^{charge}$: top-left = A, bottom-left = Z.

Spec 2.5 Isotopes

The Term 'Isotope'

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • Define isotope: atoms of the same element (same Z / protons) with different numbers of neutrons (different A)
  • State that isotopes have the same electron configuration, hence the same chemical properties
  • State that isotopes differ in physical properties that depend on mass (density, rate of diffusion)
  • Explain why $A_r$ is rarely a whole number (weighted mean of isotopes)
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The definition must include same number of protons AND different number of neutrons — "different mass number" alone is acceptable but weaker. Same chemistry because same electron arrangement — give the reason if asked.

Spec 2.6 Mass spectrometer

Mass Spectrometry — Principles & Interpreting Spectra

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals
  • Recall the four stages: vaporise → ionise (electron bombardment, forming 1+ ions) → accelerate (electric field) → deflect (magnetic field, by m/z) → detect
  • Deduce isotopic composition: each peak = one isotope; peak position = its mass; peak height/area ∝ relative abundance
  • Calculate $A_r$ from relative abundances: $A_r = \frac{\sum(\text{mass} \times \%\text{abundance})}{100}$
  • Work backwards: given $A_r$ and one abundance, find the other (two-isotope case)
  • Read the molecular ion peak M⁺ to obtain the $M_r$ of a molecule, and so identify it
  • Understand that a 2+ ion is deflected as if it had half the mass → peak at m/z = M/2
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Deflection depends on m/z, not mass alone — that is why a 2+ ion appears at half the m/z. The molecular ion peak is the highest m/z peak (ignore tiny isotope/fragment peaks beyond it). Lighter ions deflect more.

Spec 2.7 Diatomic spectra

Predicting Mass Spectra of Diatomic Molecules (Cl₂, Br₂)

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • For Cl (³⁵Cl : ³⁷Cl = 3 : 1), Cl₂ gives peaks at m/z 70, 72, 74 from ³⁵-³⁵, ³⁵-³⁷, ³⁷-³⁷
  • Relative heights follow $a^2 : 2ab : b^2$ → for 3:1, $9 : 6 : 1$
  • For Br (⁷⁹Br : ⁸¹Br ≈ 1 : 1), Br₂ gives 158, 160, 162 in ratio $1 : 2 : 1$
  • Explain the middle peak is tallest when abundances are similar (two ways to form it)
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The M+2 peak (³⁵-³⁷) has two combinations (³⁵+³⁷ and ³⁷+³⁵), hence the factor 2ab — students forget this and write 3:1:1. Peak heights reflect probability, not equal spacing.

Spec 2.8 IE definitions

First, Second & Third Ionisation Energies

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Define 1st IE: energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions
  • Define 2nd IE: from one mole of gaseous 1+ ions to 2+ ions (3rd IE from 2+ → 3+)
  • Write the equation: $\text{X(g)} \rightarrow \text{X}^+\text{(g)} + e^-$
  • State that all ionisation energies are endothermic (energy must be supplied to overcome attraction; $\Delta H$ positive)
  • Recognise units: kJ mol⁻¹
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The word gaseous is a mark — omit it and lose it. The definition is per mole. The 2nd IE removes an electron from the 1+ ion, not the neutral atom. Always endothermic — never write a negative sign.

Spec 2.9, 2.13 Orbitals & pairing

Orbitals, Spin & How Electrons Fill (Hund's Rule)

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • Define an orbital as a region within an atom that can hold up to two electrons
  • State that two electrons in the same orbital must have opposite spins
  • Apply Hund's rule: orbitals in a sub-shell each take one electron first (parallel spins) before any pairing
  • Draw electron-in-boxes diagrams showing single occupancy then pairing with opposite arrows
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

"Up to two" — an orbital can hold 0, 1 or 2 electrons. Paired electrons must be drawn as opposite arrows (↑↓), not parallel. For nitrogen the three 2p boxes are ↑ ↑ ↑ (all single), not ↑↓ ↑ _.

Spec 2.10 Factors on IE

What Controls Ionisation Energy — The Three Factors

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction → higher IE
  • Shielding: more inner electron shells → outer electron shielded from nucleus → lower IE
  • Distance: outer electron in a higher shell → further from nucleus → weaker attraction → lower IE
  • Sub-shell: an electron in a higher-energy sub-shell (e.g. 2p vs 2s) or a spin-paired electron is easier to remove → lower IE (explains the Be→B and N→O dips)
  • Explain that trends are the balance of these factors, not any one alone
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Across a period, shielding is roughly constant (same shell) so nuclear charge dominates — say so explicitly. The N→O drop is due to spin-pair repulsion in the doubly-occupied 2p orbital, not "more protons". See the Explanation Builder for the full sentence chains.

Spec 2.11 Evidence from IE

How Ionisation Energies Reveal Shells & Sub-shells

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • Successive IE: a large jump occurs when an electron is removed from a shell closer to the nucleus → the position of the jump reveals the number of outer electrons → the group
  • The grouping of successive IE values into clusters provides evidence for quantum shells
  • The pattern of first IE across a period (general rise with small dips at groups 3 and 6) provides evidence for sub-shells (s and p)
  • Read a successive-IE graph and deduce the group of the element
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Count the small values before the big jump = number of outer electrons = group (for s/p). The jump itself is because the next electron comes from a full inner shell (less shielding, closer to nucleus). Plot successive IE on a log scale to see the jumps clearly.

Spec 2.12 Orbital shapes

Shapes of s and p Orbitals

🎯 3 Key Learning Goals
  • Draw / describe the s orbital as spherical
  • Draw / describe the p orbital as dumb-bell / two-lobed
  • State there are three p orbitals ($p_x, p_y, p_z$) oriented at right angles along the x, y, z axes
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

A p orbital is one dumb-bell (two lobes), not two separate orbitals. The three p orbitals are mutually perpendicular. The s orbital has no directional lobes — it is a sphere centred on the nucleus.

Spec 2.14 Configuration

Electronic Configuration (s, p, d) & Boxes — H to Kr

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Fill in order of energy: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p (4s fills before 3d)
  • Write full and shorthand (noble-gas core) configurations for atoms H → Kr
  • Write configurations of ions: add electrons to / remove from the outermost sub-shell (for transition metals remove 4s before 3d)
  • Draw electron-in-boxes notation obeying Hund's rule and opposite spins
  • Recall the exceptions Cr ([Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹) and Cu ([Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹) — half-full / full d sub-shell stability
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

4s fills before 3d but empties first when forming ions — e.g. Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not 3d⁴ 4s². Cr and Cu are the two classic exceptions; writing the "expected" config loses the mark. Superscripts in a sub-shell must not exceed s² p⁶ d¹⁰.

Spec 2.15, 2.16 Blocks & properties

Configuration → Properties; s, p, d Blocks & Sub-shell Capacities

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Explain that electronic configuration determines chemical properties — elements in the same group have the same outer-shell configuration, hence similar chemistry
  • Know the Periodic Table is divided into s, p and d blocks, named by the sub-shell being filled
  • State sub-shell capacities: s = 2, p = 6, d = 10 electrons
  • State the maximum electrons in the first four quantum shells: 2, 8, 18, 32
  • Relate an element's block and group to its outer configuration
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The block is the sub-shell receiving the last electron, not the highest-energy one written. Helium is in the s block (1s²) despite sitting above the noble gases. Shell capacities come from $2n^2$ (n = 1→4 gives 2, 8, 18, 32).

Spec 2.17 Periodic property

Graphical Data, log(IE) & the Meaning of 'Periodic Property'

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • Represent data graphically, including log of first IE against atomic number for elements 1–36
  • Identify the recurring peaks (noble gases) and troughs (group 1) on the log(IE) graph
  • Define a periodic property: a property that shows a recurring / repeating pattern when the elements are arranged in order of atomic number
  • Use the graph as evidence that the pattern repeats each period (hence "periodic")
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The definition must mention arrangement by atomic number (not mass) and a repeating / recurring pattern — both phrases are marks. A log scale is used because IE values span a huge range; the pattern shape is what matters.

Spec 2.18 Trends (explain)

Explaining the Trends — mp/bp & Ionisation Energy

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • mp/bp across Periods 2 & 3: explain in terms of structure type (giant metallic → giant covalent → simple molecular) and the bonding / forces overcome
  • Metals Na→Al: rising mp as charge & number of delocalised electrons increase → stronger metallic bonding
  • Si (giant covalent) very high; P, S, Cl, Ar (simple molecular) low, with van der Waals forces increasing with molecule size / electron count (S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂)
  • IE across a period: general increase (nuclear charge up, shielding ~constant, same shell) with dips at group 3 (electron in higher p sub-shell) and group 6 (spin-pair repulsion)
  • IE down a group: decrease — more shells → more shielding and greater distance → weaker attraction, which outweighs the extra protons
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

This is the #1 explanation topic — see the Explanation Builder (Tab 5) for the exact sentence chains. For mp/bp you must name the structure type and the force overcome; "more electrons = higher mp" is not enough for metals (mention charge & delocalised electrons). For IE down a group the word "outweighs" is the link that wins the mark.

Out of scope in this studio: Detailed transition-metal chemistry and complex ion formation (later units); quantitative Born–Haber / lattice energy (WCH13 Topic 8); full redox & oxidation-number rules (treated as needed in Topic 1 / WCH11 §2 equations). These build on Topic 2 foundations but are taught later.

The Three Factors — Which Way Does IE Move?

Nuclear charge (protons)

More protons → stronger pull on outer e⁻ → IE up

Dominates across a period.

Shielding (inner shells)

More inner shells → outer e⁻ screened → IE down

~Constant across a period; grows down a group.

Distance / sub-shell

Higher shell or higher-energy sub-shell, or spin-paired e⁻ → easier to remove → IE down

Causes the Be→B and N→O dips.

Reading successive IE: small values = outer-shell electrons; a big jump = you have reached a full inner shell. Count the small values before the jump = outer electrons = group (for s/p elements).

Explanation Builder

Knowledge is not the problem in "explain the trend" questions — structure and wording are. This tab breaks every explanation into its causal chain and the exact phrases that earn marks. Work from the model down to writing your own.

00

The Anatomy of a 3-Mark Explanation

Examiners award one mark per logical link in a cause → effect chain. A list of true facts with no connectives scores poorly. Build every answer as three linked statements using the words because / so / therefore / which means.

LINK 1 — the cause

Name the factor that changes (e.g. number of shells / nuclear charge / structure type).

LINK 2 — the mechanism

State its effect on attraction / forces (shielding, distance, bonding strength).

LINK 3 — the consequence

Connect to the quantity asked (energy needed / temperature / rate) with therefore.

💡 The two lost-mark killers

(1) For melting/boiling point you must name the structure type (giant metallic / giant covalent / simple molecular) and the force overcome — "more electrons" alone never scores. (2) For IE down a group the extra protons and the extra shielding act in opposite directions — you must say the shielding + distance effect outweighs the extra nuclear charge.

Sentence Frame — melting point across Period 3

The hardest explanation. Reveal the structural wording piece by piece.

Na, Mg and Al have a giant metallic structure; melting requires overcoming the metallic bonding, which strengthens from Na to Al as the ion charge and the number of delocalised electrons increase, so the melting point rises. Si has a giant covalent structure with strong covalent bonds to break, giving a very high melting point. P, S, Cl and Ar are simple molecular, held by weak van der Waals forces, so little energy is needed and the melting point is low.

Model Answer Deconstruction — mp across Period 3

Read the model answer, then toggle each sentence to see exactly which mark point and which connective it earns.

[1] Sodium, magnesium and aluminium have giant metallic structures, so melting them requires overcoming the strong metallic bonding between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

Mark = names structure type (giant metallic) AND the force overcome (metallic bonding). The word "so" links structure → force.

[2] From Na to Al the charge on the ion and the number of delocalised electrons both increase, therefore the metallic bonding is stronger and the melting point rises.

Mark = the cause of the rise (charge + delocalised e⁻) linked by "therefore" to stronger bonding → higher mp. Avoid just "more electrons".

[3] Silicon has a giant covalent (macromolecular) structure, so a very high temperature is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds.

Mark = correct structure (giant covalent) + correct force (covalent bonds, which are broken, not van der Waals). This explains the peak.

[4] Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon are simple molecular, held together only by weak van der Waals forces, so little energy is needed and their melting points are low.

Mark = simple molecular + weak van der Waals forces overcome (NOT the covalent bonds within the molecules). This explains the drop after Si.

Write Your Own — then self-mark

Type your explanation, then tick the mark points you genuinely included. Reveal the model only after.

Prompt A [3 marks]: Explain the decrease in first ionisation energy down Group 1.

self-awarded: 0 / 3
Down the group, each element has one more electron shell, giving greater shielding, and the outer electron is further from the nucleus, so the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker. This outweighs the increase in nuclear charge, therefore less energy is needed to remove the outer electron and the first ionisation energy decreases.

Prompt B [2 marks]: Explain why sulfur (S₈) has a higher melting point than phosphorus (P₄), even though both are simple molecular.

self-awarded: 0 / 2
Both are simple molecular, so only van der Waals forces are overcome on melting. S₈ is a larger molecule with more electrons than P₄, so the van der Waals forces between its molecules are stronger and more energy is needed to overcome them — hence the higher melting point. (Note: it is NOT the S–S covalent bonds that are broken.)

Definitions Particles & MS Configuration IE & Trends Master Traps
Key Definitions
  • Isotope: atoms of the same element (same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons.
  • 1st ionisation energy: energy to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms → 1 mol gaseous 1+ ions (kJ mol⁻¹, endothermic).
  • Orbital: a region within an atom that can hold up to two electrons (with opposite spins).
  • Periodic property: a property showing a recurring pattern when elements are arranged in order of atomic number.
  • Atomic number Z: number of protons. Mass number A: protons + neutrons.
    • Particles & Mass Spectrometry

    protons = Z · neutrons = A − Z · electrons = Z − charge

    Ar = Σ(mass × %abundance) / 100

    Mass spec stages: vaporise → ionise (e⁻ bombardment) → accelerate (E-field) → deflect (B-field, by m/z) → detect.

    Molecular ion peak M⁺ = highest m/z = Mr of molecule. A 2+ ion appears at m/z = M/2.

    Diatomic X₂ peak heights = a² : 2ab : b² (Cl₂ with 3:1 → 9:6:1; Br₂ with 1:1 → 1:2:1).

    Electronic Configuration
  • Fill order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p. Capacities s=2, p=6, d=10. Shell n = 2n².
  • Hund's rule: fill each orbital in a sub-shell singly (parallel spins) before pairing; paired = opposite spins.
  • Ions: add/remove from outermost sub-shell; transition metals lose 4s before 3d (Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶).
  • Exceptions: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹; Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
  • Blocks s/p/d named by sub-shell being filled; He is s block. Same outer config → same group → similar chemistry.
    • Ionisation Energy & Trends (the three explanation chains)

    IE across a period (general rise): nuclear charge increases, shielding ~constant (same shell) → stronger attraction → more energy to remove e⁻. Dips: group 3 (e⁻ in higher-energy p sub-shell) and group 6 (spin-pair repulsion).

    IE down a group (decrease): more shells → more shielding + greater distance → weaker attraction, which outweighs extra protons → less energy needed.

    mp/bp across Period 3: giant metallic (Na–Al, rising: charge + delocalised e⁻) → giant covalent (Si, peak) → simple molecular (P–Ar, low: weak van der Waals).

    Successive IE: big jump = new inner shell; count small values before jump = group (s/p).

    Master Trap Checklist

    □ IE definition missing the word "gaseous" or "per mole"

    □ 2nd IE written as removing from the neutral atom (must be from 1+)

    □ Ion electron count = Z (forgot to adjust for charge)

    □ Electron relative mass written as exactly 0 (it is ≈ 0)

    □ Isotope definition missing "same protons / different neutrons"

    □ 2+ ion peak not placed at m/z = M/2

    □ Cl₂ middle peak ratio wrong (must be 2ab → 9:6:1, not 9:3:1)

    □ N's 2p boxes drawn as ↑↓ ↑ _ instead of ↑ ↑ ↑

    □ 4s not emptied before 3d for transition-metal ions

    □ Cr / Cu written with the "expected" (wrong) configuration

    □ He placed in p block (it is s block, 1s²)

    □ IE across period: forgot "shielding ~constant, same shell"

    □ N→O dip blamed on "more protons" (it is spin-pair repulsion)

    □ IE down group: missing the word "outweighs"

    □ mp explanation: no structure type named / no force named

    □ Simple-molecular melting said to break covalent bonds (it breaks van der Waals)

    □ Periodic-property definition missing "atomic number" or "recurring pattern"

    3D Orbital Boundary Surfaces

    s orbitals (size grows with n)

    p orbitals (dumbbell, along an axis)

    Overlap views

    s Orbital

    Spherical probability cloud centred on the nucleus. No directional preference.

    ↻ drag to rotate · an orbital is a region that holds up to 2 e⁻ with opposite spins

    1 · Aufbau Principle

    Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p. Lower-energy sub-shells fill first. Note 4s fills before 3d but empties first when forming ions.

    2 · Hund's Rule

    Electrons occupy degenerate (equal-energy) orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing. E.g. fill all three 2p orbitals with one electron each before any pairing. This minimises repulsion.

    3 · Pauli Exclusion

    Each orbital holds a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins (+½ and −½). Never draw two electrons with the same spin in one orbital.
