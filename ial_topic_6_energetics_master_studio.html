Spec 6.1 - 6.2 ΔH & Standard Conditions

1. Enthalpy Change & the Sign Convention

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define $\Delta H$ as the heat energy change measured at constant pressure.
  • State standard conditions: 100 kPa pressure and a stated temperature, usually 298 K ($25^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Exothermic: $\Delta H$ negative — heat released to the surroundings; products have lower enthalpy than reactants.
  • Endothermic: $\Delta H$ positive — heat taken in from the surroundings; products have higher enthalpy.
  • Recognise the plimsoll symbol $\ominus$ means "under standard conditions" (e.g. $\Delta_\text{c}H^\ominus$).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Standard pressure is 100 kPa — not 101.3 kPa or "1 atm" (older values lose the mark). The sign of ΔH is a mark point: writing "ΔH = 54 kJ mol⁻¹" for an exothermic change scores zero — it must be −54 kJ mol⁻¹. Always give units of kJ mol⁻¹.

Spec 6.3 Enthalpy Level Diagrams

2. Enthalpy Level Diagrams

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Draw an exothermic diagram: reactants line above products line; $\Delta H$ arrow points down (negative).
  • Draw an endothermic diagram: products line above reactants; $\Delta H$ arrow points up (positive).
  • Label the y-axis "Enthalpy, $H$" and label each level with the species (with state symbols).
  • The $\Delta H$ arrow runs from the reactants level to the products level, labelled with value, sign and units.
  • Distinguish these diagrams from reaction profiles — enthalpy level diagrams show no activation-energy hump.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Drawing a "hump" (activation energy) on an enthalpy level diagram is a contradiction examiners flag. If a value is given, the arrow must carry sign + value + units (e.g. $-286\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$). For multi-mole equations, the value refers to the equation as written.

Spec 6.4 Five Standard Enthalpy Changes

3. $\Delta_\text{r}H$, $\Delta_\text{f}H$, $\Delta_\text{c}H$, $\Delta_\text{neut}H$, $\Delta_\text{at}H$

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • $\Delta_\text{r}H$: enthalpy change when molar amounts of reactants react as written in the equation, under standard conditions.
  • $\Delta_\text{f}H$: formation of 1 mole of a compound from its elements, all in standard states (e.g. $\text{C(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CH}_4\text{(g)}$).
  • $\Delta_\text{c}H$: complete combustion of 1 mole of substance in excess oxygen (e.g. C to $\text{CO}_2$, H to $\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$).
  • $\Delta_\text{neut}H$: formation of 1 mole of water from the neutralisation of an acid and an alkali in dilute solution.
  • $\Delta_\text{at}H$: formation of 1 mole of gaseous atoms from the element in its standard state — always positive (e.g. $\tfrac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{Cl(g)}$).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

$\Delta_\text{f}H$ of any element in its standard state is zero ($\text{O}_2\text{(g)}$, $\text{C(s, graphite)}$). Formation equations must form exactly 1 mole of product — fractions such as $\tfrac{1}{2}\text{O}_2$ are required and allowed. Combustion must say complete and excess O₂; neutralisation is per mole of H₂O formed, so $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ equations need $\tfrac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

Spec 6.5 Calorimetry Calculations

4. $Q = mc\Delta T$ and $\Delta H$ from Experiment

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Recall energy transferred (J) = mass (g) × specific heat capacity (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹) × temperature change (°C).
  • Use $c = 4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{ °C}^{-1}$ for dilute aqueous solutions and assume density $1.00\text{ g cm}^{-3}$.
  • Convert $Q$ from J to kJ (÷ 1000), then $\Delta H = \pm Q / n$ where $n$ = moles of the limiting reagent.
  • For mixing experiments use the total volume of the mixture (acid + alkali) as the mass.
  • Combustion: heat released by the fuel = heat gained by the water; $\Delta_\text{c}H = -Q / n(\text{fuel})$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Forgetting ÷ 1000 (J → kJ) is the single most common calorimetry error — answers of "−54 300 kJ mol⁻¹" score zero. Temperature rise ⇒ exothermic ⇒ negative ΔH. Mark schemes accept e.g. "$Q = 200 \times 4.18 \times 40 = 33\,440\text{ J}$" style working — show every step.

Spec 6.6 - 6.7 Hess's Law & Core Practical 2

5. Hess's Law, Enthalpy Cycles & CP2

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State Hess's Law: the total enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided initial and final conditions are the same.
  • Formation-route cycle: $\Delta_\text{r}H = \sum \Delta_\text{f}H(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta_\text{f}H(\text{reactants})$.
  • Combustion-route cycle: $\Delta_\text{r}H = \sum \Delta_\text{c}H(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta_\text{c}H(\text{products})$.
  • CP2: determine $\Delta H$ for $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$ indirectly, by reacting both $\text{CaCO}_3$ and $\text{CaO}$ with excess dilute HCl and measuring each $\Delta T$.
  • The direct route is impossible: decomposition needs very high temperature and the energy change cannot be measured in school apparatus.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Do not swap the two formulae: formation = products − reactants, but combustion = reactants − products (follow the downward arrows in the cycle). When an equation is reversed, change the sign of ΔH; when multiplied, multiply ΔH too. Elements sit at the base of a formation cycle with $\Delta_\text{f}H = 0$.

Spec 6.8 Evaluation, Error & Uncertainty

6. Evaluating Calorimetry Results

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Name the key error sources: heat loss to the surroundings / calorimeter, incomplete combustion, evaporation of fuel or volatile reactants, non-standard conditions.
  • Explain the consequence: smaller measured $\Delta T$ ⇒ smaller $Q$ ⇒ experimental $\Delta_\text{c}H$ less exothermic than the data-booklet value.
  • Cooling-curve correction: plot temperature against time and extrapolate the pre-mixing and cooling lines back to the time of mixing to obtain the true $\Delta T$ at $t = 0$ of mixing.
  • Suggest improvements: polystyrene cup with lid, insulation, stirring, draught shield and spirit-burner lid for combustion.
  • State the assumptions: solution density $1.00\text{ g cm}^{-3}$, specific heat capacity equal to water's, negligible calorimeter heat capacity, no heat loss.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"Heat loss" alone is a bare phrase — full credit needs the chain: heat loss ⇒ smaller ΔT ⇒ smaller Q ⇒ ΔH less exothermic. Mark schemes rank heat loss as the dominant error, above "non-standard conditions". The extrapolated (corrected) ΔT is always larger than the observed maximum rise.

Spec 6.9 - 6.10 Bond Enthalpy

7. Bond Enthalpies & Mean Bond Enthalpies

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define bond enthalpy: the energy required to break 1 mole of a given bond in the gaseous state, homolytically — always positive (bond breaking is endothermic).
  • Define mean bond enthalpy: the average value for that bond taken over a range of compounds (e.g. C–H differs slightly in CH₄ vs C₂H₆).
  • Calculate $\Delta_\text{r}H = \sum E(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum E(\text{bonds formed})$.
  • Know the limitations: all species must be gaseous, and values are averages, so results are approximate.
  • Rearrange the cycle to find an unknown mean bond enthalpy from a known $\Delta_\text{r}H$ (Spec 6.10).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

It is broken − formed, not formed − broken. Count bonds from the displayed formula: CO₂ contains two C=O bonds, CH₄ contains four C–H bonds, O₂ is O=O. If water is a liquid product, bond-enthalpy calculation does not strictly apply (state the limitation for the mark).

Spec 6.11 Bond Enthalpy & Reactivity

8. Which Bond Breaks First?

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Bond enthalpy data indicates which bond breaks first and how easily: the weaker (lower enthalpy) bond breaks preferentially.
  • Apply to halogenoalkanes (Topic 10 link): C–F 467 > C–Cl 346 > C–Br 290 > C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹, so C–I breaks most easily.
  • Hence the rate of hydrolysis increases R–I > R–Br > R–Cl — bond strength controls the rate at room temperature.
  • Breaking a bond is always endothermic; forming the same bond releases exactly the same magnitude of energy (exothermic).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never write "stronger bonds release more energy when broken" — breaking never releases energy. Do not confuse bond enthalpy with activation energy: bond enthalpy is a thermodynamic quantity; $E_\text{a}$ is kinetic (Topic 9A). C–F is the strongest carbon–halogen bond, so fluoroalkanes are the most inert.

Entropy ($\Delta S$), total entropy & feasibility, lattice energy, Born–Haber cycles, $\Delta_\text{sol}H$ and $\Delta_\text{hyd}H$ are NOT in Topic 6. They belong to Topic 12: Entropy and Energetics (WCH14, Paper 5) — see the companion studios unit12a_entropy and unit12b_lattice_energy. Activation energy and reaction profiles belong to Topic 9A Kinetics (companion studio unit_9a_kinetics).

Engine A · Syllabus 6.1–6.5

Enthalpy Change & Calorimetry Learning Suite

6 concept cards
1. ΔH & standard conditions (6.1–6.2)
$\Delta H$ = heat energy change at constant pressure · standard: 100 kPa, usually 298 K

Exothermic: $\Delta H < 0$. Energy released as bonds in products are stronger overall than in reactants. Surroundings warm up.

Endothermic: $\Delta H > 0$. Energy absorbed. Surroundings cool down (e.g. dissolving $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$).

Learning goals
  • State the definition of ΔH including "constant pressure".
  • Give both standard conditions exactly (100 kPa; stated temperature, usually 298 K).
  • Link sign of ΔH to direction of heat flow and relative enthalpy of products.
Examiner tips
  • "Heat change" alone is incomplete — "at constant pressure" is the mark point.
  • Do not write 273 K or "room temperature" — write 298 K.
  • The sign is always a mark: exothermic must be shown negative.
2. Enthalpy level diagrams (6.3)

Y-axis: Enthalpy, $H$. The ΔH arrow runs from the reactants level to the products level and is labelled with sign, value and units.

Learning goals
  • Sketch both diagram types with correct relative levels and arrow direction.
  • Label axis, levels (with state symbols) and the ΔH arrow fully.
  • Explain why no activation-energy hump appears here.
Examiner tips
  • A hump on an enthalpy level diagram contradicts the diagram type — omit it.
  • Unlabelled arrows lose the mark even if the shape is right.

  • State that ΔfH of an element in its standard state = 0.

4. The calorimetry equation (6.5i)
$Q\ (\text{J}) = m\ (\text{g}) \times c\ (\text{J g}^{-1}\text{°C}^{-1}) \times \Delta T\ (\text{°C})$

m: mass of the substance heated — for solutions, total volume in cm³ = mass in g (density $1.00\text{ g cm}^{-3}$).

c: $4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{°C}^{-1}$ for dilute aqueous solutions (value for water).

ΔT: temperature change — rise for exothermic, fall for endothermic. Use the corrected value from a cooling curve where required.

Learning goals
  • Substitute into Q = mcΔT with correct units and total mass.
  • Convert J → kJ before dividing by moles.
  • Attach the correct sign to ΔH from the direction of ΔT.
Examiner tips
  • Mark scheme style: "Q = 200 × 4.18 × 40 = 33 440 J" — show substitution explicitly.
  • Using only one solution's volume instead of the total is a classic slip.
5. Worked example — neutralisation (6.5ii)

Problem: 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH in a polystyrene cup. Temperature rises by 6.5 °C. Calculate $\Delta_\text{neut}H$.

$m = 25.0 + 25.0 = 50.0\text{ g}$

$Q = 50.0 \times 4.18 \times 6.5 = 1358.5\text{ J} = 1.3585\text{ kJ}$

$n(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 0.0250\text{ dm}^3 \times 1.00 = 0.0250\text{ mol}$

$\Delta_\text{neut}H = -1.3585 / 0.0250 = \mathbf{-54.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

The data-booklet value for strong acid + strong alkali is about $-57\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$; the experimental value is less exothermic mainly because of heat loss.

Learning goals
  • Reproduce the four-step method: mass → Q → kJ → per mole with sign.
  • Explain why the answer is less exothermic than −57.
Examiner tips
  • Give the final answer to 3 significant figures with sign and units.
  • If one reagent is in excess, moles of water are set by the limiting reagent.
6. Worked example — combustion of ethanol (6.5ii)

Problem: Burning 0.50 g of ethanol heats 100 g of water by 29.0 °C. Calculate $\Delta_\text{c}H$ for ethanol ($M_\text{r} = 46.0$).

$Q = 100 \times 4.18 \times 29.0 = 12\,122\text{ J} = 12.122\text{ kJ}$

$n(\text{ethanol}) = 0.50 / 46.0 = 0.01087\text{ mol}$

$\Delta_\text{c}H = -12.122 / 0.01087 = \mathbf{-1115\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Data-booklet value: $-1367\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. The experimental value is less exothermic — heat loss to the surroundings, incomplete combustion and evaporation of ethanol all reduce the measured ΔT.

Learning goals
  • Set up the combustion calculation: Q gained by water = energy released by fuel.
  • Evaluate the result against the data-booklet value and name the dominant error.
Examiner tips
  • Rank the errors: heat loss dominates; "non-standard conditions" is a minor, last-credit point.
  • Improvements: draught shield, lid on spirit burner when weighing, closer flame-to-can distance.

Engine B · Syllabus 6.6–6.8

Hess's Law & Enthalpy Cycles Learning Suite

5 concept cards
1. Hess's Law (6.6)
Total $\Delta H$ is route-independent — same start, same finish, same $\Delta H$

Why it works: enthalpy is a state function — it depends only on the initial and final states, never on the path.

Use: calculate ΔH for reactions that cannot be measured directly (e.g. formation of CO from incomplete combustion, decomposition of CaCO₃).

Learning goals
  • State Hess's Law in full exam wording.
  • Explain why indirect routes are needed for some reactions.
Examiner tips
  • "Independent of the route" must be paired with "same initial and final conditions" for full marks.
  • Never say Hess's Law is about energy conservation of the surroundings — keep it about the reaction's ΔH.
2. Formation-route cycles (6.6i–ii)
$\Delta_\text{r}H = \sum \Delta_\text{f}H(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta_\text{f}H(\text{reactants})$

Worked example: $\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$ with $\Delta_\text{f}H$: CH₄ = −75, CO₂ = −394, H₂O(l) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹.

$\Delta_\text{r}H = [\Delta_\text{f}H(\text{CO}_2) + 2\Delta_\text{f}H(\text{H}_2\text{O})] - [\Delta_\text{f}H(\text{CH}_4) + 2\Delta_\text{f}H(\text{O}_2)]$

$= [-394 + 2(-286)] - [-75 + 0] = -966 + 75 = \mathbf{-891\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

In the cycle, elements in their standard states form the base line; arrows rise from elements to each compound.

Learning goals
  • Draw the formation cycle with elements at the base.
  • Apply products − reactants with stoichiometric multipliers.
Examiner tips
  • ΔfH(O₂) = 0 — students who insert a value here lose the mark.
  • Watch the double negative: subtracting −75 becomes +75.
3. Combustion-route cycles (6.6i–ii)
$\Delta_\text{r}H = \sum \Delta_\text{c}H(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta_\text{c}H(\text{products})$

Worked example: find $\Delta_\text{f}H$ of CH₄ from $\Delta_\text{c}H$: C = −394, H₂ = −286, CH₄ = −891 kJ mol⁻¹ for $\text{C(s)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CH}_4\text{(g)}$.

$\Delta_\text{f}H = [\Delta_\text{c}H(\text{C}) + 2\Delta_\text{c}H(\text{H}_2)] - \Delta_\text{c}H(\text{CH}_4)$

$= [-394 + 2(-286)] - (-891) = -966 + 891 = \mathbf{-75\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Here the combustion products (CO₂ + H₂O) form the base line; arrows fall from each substance down to them.

Learning goals
  • Draw the combustion cycle with combustion products at the base.
  • Apply reactants − products and explain why the order is reversed versus formation.
Examiner tips
  • The safest method: follow the arrows. Down-left + across = down-right, reading signs from arrow direction.
  • Swapping to products − reactants in a combustion cycle is the most common Hess error in scripts.
4. Core Practical 2 — CaCO₃ decomposition (6.7)
Target: $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s)} + \text{CO}_2\text{(g)}$ — $\Delta H_1$ cannot be measured directly

Measured routes (both with excess dilute HCl, polystyrene cup):

$\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 \quad \Delta H_2 \approx -15\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

$\text{CaO} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \Delta H_3 \approx -196\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$

$\Delta H_1 = \Delta H_2 - \Delta H_3 = -15 - (-196) = \mathbf{+181\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$ (endothermic ✓; literature ≈ +178)

Direct heating of CaCO₃ needs ~900 °C — impossible to measure the energy change with school calorimetry, hence the indirect Hess route.

Learning goals
  • Draw the CP2 cycle and derive ΔH₁ = ΔH₂ − ΔH₃.
  • Explain why the direct route is impractical.
  • Carry out the two calorimetry calculations from ΔT data.
Examiner tips
  • Use excess HCl so all solid reacts — moles are then based on the solid's mass.
  • The final answer must be positive (endothermic decomposition) — a negative result signals a sign slip.
5. Manipulating equations (6.6ii)

Rule 1 — reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH. If $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$ is −394, then $\text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{C} + \text{O}_2$ is +394 kJ mol⁻¹.

Rule 2 — multiply an equation: multiply ΔH by the same factor. Doubling the equation doubles ΔH.

Rule 3 — add equations: add the ΔH values, cancelling species that appear on both sides.

Example: $\text{C} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}$ cannot be measured (CO always forms with some CO₂).

Use: (1) $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$ −394 and (2) $\text{CO} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$ −283.

(1) − (2): $\Delta H = -394 - (-283) = \mathbf{-111\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Learning goals
  • Apply the three manipulation rules without error.
  • Combine given equations to reach a target equation.
Examiner tips
  • Write the target equation first, then arrange the given equations to match it — a systematic method prevents sign chaos.
  • State symbols matter when cancelling (H₂O(l) ≠ H₂O(g)).

⚡ Hess cycle trap pairs
❌ Trap: one formula for both routes ✅ Truth: formation = prod − react; combustion = react − prod

The direction of the arrows in the cycle decides the subtraction order. When in doubt, trace the arrows.

❌ Trap: measuring CaCO₃ decomposition directly ✅ Truth: indirect Hess route via HCl reactions

~900 °C is needed for decomposition; school calorimeters cannot capture it. CP2 exists precisely because the direct route fails.

❌ Trap: forgetting the stoichiometric multiplier ✅ Truth: 2 × ΔfH(H₂O), 2 × ΔcH(H₂)

Every ΔH in the sum must be multiplied by the coefficient in the balanced equation.

❌ Trap: sign slip on double negatives ✅ Truth: −(−75) = +75

Write the full bracketed expression first, then simplify. Most Hess arithmetic errors happen at this step.

❌ Trap: giving ΔfH to O₂ or N₂ ✅ Truth: elements in standard states have ΔfH = 0

Inserting a value for O₂(g) in a formation sum is an instant lost mark.

❌ Trap: limiting HCl in CP2 ✅ Truth: excess HCl, moles from the solid

Excess acid guarantees complete reaction of CaCO₃ / CaO, so the energy change is per mole of solid.

Engine C · Syllabus 6.9–6.11

Bond Enthalpy Learning Suite

5 concept cards
1. Bond enthalpy definition (6.9)
Energy to break 1 mol of a bond, gaseous state, homolytic — always positive

Breaking a bond is endothermic (+); forming the same bond is exothermic (−), same magnitude.

Typical data-booklet values (kJ mol⁻¹): H–H 436 · O=O 498 · C–H 413 · C–C 348 · C=C 612 · C=O 805 · O–H 464 · N≡N 945 · N–H 391 · H–Cl 432 · Cl–Cl 243.

Learning goals
  • Write the full definition with "1 mole", "gaseous" and the positive sign.
  • State the break/form energy symmetry.
Examiner tips
  • "Gaseous state" is a mark point — bond enthalpies do not apply to liquids/solids.
  • Never describe bond breaking as releasing energy.
2. Mean bond enthalpy (6.9)

Mean bond enthalpy = the average bond-dissociation energy for a given bond type taken over a range of compounds.

Example: the C–H bond energy differs slightly between CH₄, C₂H₆ and CH₃Cl. The tabulated 413 kJ mol⁻¹ is an average — hence calculated ΔH values are approximate.

Learning goals
  • Explain the word "mean" with a concrete example.
  • Link averaging to the approximate nature of the results.
Examiner tips
  • When asked why a bond-enthalpy answer differs from a calorimetry value, give both reasons: averaging and non-gaseous states.
3. ΔrH = Σ broken − Σ formed (worked example)

Problem: estimate ΔcH for $\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$.

Broken: 4 × C–H = 4 × 413 = 1652 · 2 × O=O = 2 × 498 = 996 → Σ = 2648

Formed: 2 × C=O = 2 × 805 = 1610 · 4 × O–H = 4 × 464 = 1856 → Σ = 3466

$\Delta H = 2648 - 3466 = \mathbf{-818\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Measured ΔcH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹. The gap comes from mean values and from water being a gas in the calculation but a liquid in the standard measurement.

Learning goals
  • Count every bond from displayed formulae (CO₂ has two C=O bonds).
  • Apply broken − formed and interpret the sign.
Examiner tips
  • Lay out the working as two labelled columns (broken / formed) — method marks follow the layout.
  • A negative result for combustion is a built-in sanity check.
4. Limitations (6.9)

Limitation 1 — state: bond enthalpies apply to gaseous molecules only. If a reactant or product is liquid (e.g. H₂O(l), ethanol), the calculation ignores vaporisation energy.

Limitation 2 — averaging: mean values are averages across compounds; the true value in a specific molecule differs slightly.

Consequence: bond-enthalpy ΔH values are estimates — expect a few % difference from experimental (calorimetry or Hess) values.

Learning goals
  • State both limitations in exam language.
  • Predict which direction the error pushes the answer for a given reaction.
Examiner tips
  • "Bond enthalpies are wrong" is not an answer — name the two specific reasons.
  • This limitation question is a frequent 2-mark part attached to calculations.
5. Unknown bond enthalpy & reactivity (6.10–6.11)

Worked example (6.10): for $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{HCl}$, $\Delta_\text{r}H = -184\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$; H–H = 436, Cl–Cl = 243. Find E(H–Cl).

Broken: 436 + 243 = 679 · Formed: 2 × E(H–Cl)

$-184 = 679 - 2E \Rightarrow 2E = 863 \Rightarrow E(\text{H–Cl}) = \mathbf{431.5 \approx 432\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Reactivity (6.11): carbon–halogen bond enthalpies: C–F 467 > C–Cl 346 > C–Br 290 > C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹. The weakest bond (C–I) breaks most easily, so iodoalkanes hydrolyse fastest at room temperature — bond strength sets the rate (Topic 10 link).

Learning goals
  • Rearrange broken − formed to find one unknown bond enthalpy.
  • Use bond enthalpy data to predict which bond breaks first and relative rates.
Examiner tips
  • Remember the factor of 2 when the product contains two of the bond (2HCl ⇒ 2 × E).
  • For hydrolysis rates, the C–X bond is the one broken — quote the trend values.

⚡ Bond enthalpy trap pairs
❌ Trap: formed − broken ✅ Truth: broken − formed

Energy goes in to break (positive) and comes out when bonds form (negative): ΔH = ΣE(broken) − ΣE(formed).

❌ Trap: counting one C=O in CO₂ ✅ Truth: O=C=O has two double bonds

Draw the displayed formula before counting. The same applies to O=O and N≡N.

❌ Trap: using the method for liquids ✅ Truth: gaseous species only

For CH₄ combustion, write H₂O(g) when using bond enthalpies; the standard ΔcH uses H₂O(l). State the mismatch for the evaluation mark.

❌ Trap: "stronger bonds release more energy when broken" ✅ Truth: breaking always absorbs energy

Stronger bonds absorb more energy to break; energy is released only when bonds form.

❌ Trap: bond enthalpy = activation energy ✅ Truth: thermodynamic vs kinetic

Bond enthalpy is an energy change (ΔH territory); Ea is the kinetic barrier (Topic 9A). Related ideas, different quantities.

❌ Trap: forgetting the coefficient in 6.10 problems ✅ Truth: 2HCl ⇒ 2 × E(H–Cl)

Set up "formed = 2E" before rearranging — halving errors are common under time pressure.
