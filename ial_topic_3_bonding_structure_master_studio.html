⚠️ PEF Reminders — Principal Examiner Feedback (2019–2025) from chemistry-wiki wchNN-examiner-insights
  • Metallic bonding: “delocalised electrons that can move/flow” + electrostatic attraction between cations and the electron sea; “free electrons”/“charge carriers” too vague (Jan 2020, Jun 2024).
  • Ionic conductivity: mobile IONS in the liquid, NOT delocalised electrons; solid doesn't conduct (no mobile charge carriers) (Jan 2020, Jan 2022, Jan 2024).
  • Polarisation: small, highly charged cation → more polarising power; students reverse it or confuse polarisability with polarising power (Jun 2023).
  • BeCl₂ linear 180° (covalent, on spec); BF₃ trigonal planar 120° (no lone pairs); PCl₅ trigonal bipyramidal 90°+120°; PCl₃ trigonal pyramidal (Oct 2023, Jan 2024, Oct 2019, Jun 2023).
  • Water 104.5° (not 107°) — oxygen has 2 bp + 2 lp (Jan 2020).
  • SF₆ is 3-D octahedral and non-polar despite polar S–F bonds; lone-pair omission common (Jan 2025).
  • Al₂Cl₆ dimer: dative bond from Cl→Al (lone pair on Cl), NOT Al→Cl; no Al–Al bond (Jan 2023, Oct 2024).
  • Sigma vs pi: pi is NOT the double bond by itself; sideways vs end-on overlap (Jan 2024).
  • Electronegativity difference has NO threshold for ionic vs covalent — continuous scale; calling one purely ionic and the other purely covalent loses marks (Jun 2023).
  • Dative bond in dot-and-cross: electrons within the dative bond must appear identical (all from the same atom) (Jun 2019).

Spec 3.3 · 3.11 · PEF 2019–2024 Dot-Cross Rules

Dot-and-Cross Precision & $\text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6$ Dative Bridges

Principal Examiner Feedback (Jun 2019, Jan 2023, Oct 2024) highlights three recurring dot-and-cross mark losses:

M1 · Ionic brackets & charges: Square brackets AND outside charges are mandatory; $\text{Na}^+$ must show a full 8-electron 2nd shell (or 0 in 3rd), never shared pairs.
M2 · Dative pair symbols: In a dative bond, both electrons must share the same symbol (both dots or both crosses) because both originate from the donor atom.
M3 · $\text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6$ bridge direction: Arrow points $\text{Cl} \rightarrow \text{Al}$ (lone pair on bridging Cl into empty orbital on Al); there is no Al–Al bond.
Spec 3.6–3.9 · 3.13–3.14 PEF Jun 2023

Polarisation vs. Electronegativity Continuum

Examiner Report (Jun 2023): There is no sharp $\Delta\text{EN}$ threshold separating ionic and covalent bonding — it is a continuous spectrum:

Cation Polarising Power: High charge + small radius = high charge density ($\text{Al}^{3+} > \text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Na}^+$) → pulls anion electron cloud strongly.
Anion Polarisability: Large radius + high negative charge ($\text{I}^- > \text{Br}^- > \text{Cl}^- > \text{F}^-$) → outer electrons easily distorted.
Electron Density Maps: Ideal ionic = separate spheres; polarised ionic = distorted anion contours; covalent = shared density between nuclei.
Spec 3.15–3.19 · PEF Jan 2025 VSEPR & Polarity

VSEPR Repulsion Order & Dipole Cancellation

Examiner Reports (Jan 2020, Oct 2023, Jan 2025) emphasise quoting the exact repulsion hierarchy and 3D symmetry:

Exact Angle Series: 4 electron pairs: $\text{CH}_4 / \text{NH}_4^+$ (4 bp, 0 lp = $109.5^\circ$) $\rightarrow$ $\text{NH}_3 / \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ (3 bp, 1 lp = $107^\circ$) $\rightarrow$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (2 bp, 2 lp = $104.5^\circ$).
Electron-Deficient & Expanded: $\text{BeCl}_2$ linear $180^\circ$ (2 bp, 0 lp); $\text{BF}_3 / \text{BCl}_3$ trigonal planar $120^\circ$ (3 bp, 0 lp); $\text{PCl}_5(g)$ $90^\circ \& 120^\circ$; $\text{SF}_6$ $90^\circ$.
Solid vs Gaseous $\text{PCl}_5$: $\text{PCl}_5(g)$ is trigonal bipyramidal, whereas $\text{PCl}_5(s)$ is ionic: $[\text{PCl}_4]^+$ ($109.5^\circ$) and $[\text{PCl}_6]^-$ ($90^\circ$).

📝 Mark-Scheme Word Bank — Ionic Bonding (Spec 3.1–3.9)

Ionic bond definition (2 marks)

① Strong electrostatic attraction ② between oppositely charged ions (in a giant ionic lattice, acting in all directions).

Conductivity evidence (2 marks)

Solid does not conduct because ions are fixed in the lattice; molten/aqueous conducts because ions are free to move and carry charge (never say electrons!).

Polarising power vs Polarisability (3 marks)

Small, highly charged cation has high polarising power (high charge density) and distorts the electron cloud of a large, highly charged polarisable anion.

Isoelectronic series N³⁻ → Al³⁺ (3 marks)

① All have 10 electrons / same electron configuration (1s² 2s² 2p⁶) ② nuclear charge (protons) increases from 7 to 13 ③ outer electrons pulled closer to nucleus.

📝 Mark-Scheme Word Bank — Covalent Bonding & Polarity (Spec 3.10–3.15)

Covalent bond definition (2 marks)

Strong electrostatic attraction between the shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the two bonded atoms.

Dative covalent bond (2 marks)

A covalent bond in which both shared electrons come from the same atom (e.g. lone pair on Cl donated into empty orbital on Al in $\text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6$).

Electronegativity definition (2 marks)

The ability of an atom to attract the bonding / shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

Non-polar molecule with polar bonds (2 marks)

Bonds are polar due to $\Delta\text{EN}$, but the molecule is symmetrical (linear / trigonal planar / tetrahedral / octahedral) so the bond dipoles cancel out.

Shape & Polarity Predictor

Knowing the shapes is not the problem — deriving them and judging polarity is. Sketch the molecule, read off the geometry and angle, then decide whether the bond dipoles cancel. Finish with the causal-chain builders for the property explanations.

00

The Two-Step Method

STEP 1 — the shape

Count electron-pair regions on the central atom (bonding pairs + lone pairs; a multiple bond = one region; adjust for ion charge). Match the (bp, lp) pair to a geometry; reduce the angle for each lone pair.

STEP 2 — the polarity

Put δ+/δ− on each bond (δ− on the more electronegative atom). If the shape is symmetrical with identical terminal atoms, the dipoles cancel → non-polar; otherwise polar.

💡 Repulsion order to quote

lone–lone > lone–bonding > bonding–bonding. A lone pair is "fatter" and repels more, squeezing the bonding pairs together — that is why 109.5° → 107° (NH₃) → 104.5° (H₂O).

🧪 WCH13 Practical Link — Bonding & Structure

Conductivity as evidence

Ionic compounds conduct when molten/aqueous (ions free to move), not as solids. Metallic solids conduct (delocalised electrons). WCH13 tests this via circuit + bulb.

Melting point evidence

High mp + conducts molten → ionic lattice; low mp + poor conductor → simple molecular. Measure, then explain via structure + forces.

Flame test cross-link

Cation flame tests (Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, etc.) link electronic structure to colour — see Topic 2, Tab 12.

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Bonding & Structure

Metallic bonding wording

“Electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.” Never “shared electrons”, “intermolecular” or “molecules”.

Dot-and-cross charges

Ionic dot-cross MUST show charges on the ions — missing brackets, shared pairs or charge labels = covalent = 0 marks.

Polarisation direction

Small radius + high charge → MORE polarising power. Students reverse it or confuse with polarisability.

VSEPR angles

Linear 180°; trigonal planar 120°; pyramidal ~107°; bent ~104.5°. Never “square planar” for 2 bonding pairs.

Melting point Be > Ba

Smaller ion → higher charge density → stronger electrostatic attraction. Not “more electrons” / “electronegativity”.

Dative bond Al₂Cl₆

Lone pair on Cl → empty orbital on Al. Not “covalent between Al atoms” or “ionic dimer”.

Key Definitions
  • Ionic bonding: the strong net electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice.
  • Covalent bonding: the strong electrostatic attraction between two nuclei and the shared pair of electrons between them.
  • Dative (coordinate) bond: a covalent bond in which both shared electrons come from one atom.
  • Metallic bonding: the strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Polarisation: distortion of an anion's electron cloud by a cation, giving partial covalent character.
  • Isoelectronic: species with the same number of electrons (same electron configuration).
  • Bond length / bond angle: nucleus–nucleus distance / angle between two bonds at a central atom.
    • Ionic — Rules & Facts
  • Evidence for ions: high mp; conducts only molten/aq; electron-density maps show separate spheres; coloured ions migrate to electrodes.
  • Strength ↑ with larger ionic charge and smaller ionic radius (charge effect usually dominates).
  • Ionic radius ↑ down a group (extra shell). Isoelectronic N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺ (most protons = smallest).
  • Polarising power (cation) ↑ with high charge + small radius. Polarisability (anion) ↑ with large radius + high charge.
  • Most covalent character: small highly-charged cation + large highly-charged anion (Al³⁺/Cl⁻ → Al₂Cl₆).
    • Covalent & Polarity — Rules & Facts
  • Evidence for covalent bonding: high mp of giant atomic structures; electron-density maps show density between nuclei.
  • Diamond (4 bonds, insulator, hard), graphite (3 bonds + delocalised e⁻ between layers, conducts, lubricant), graphene (one layer, strong, conducts).
  • Ionic ↔ covalent is a continuum set by ΔEN: ≈0 non-polar covalent, moderate polar covalent, large ionic.
  • Polar bond ≠ polar molecule. Symmetrical shapes with identical terminals cancel dipoles → non-polar (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄). Asymmetrical / lone pairs → polar (H₂O, NH₃, OF₂).

    Metallic & the Four Structure Types
  • Giant ionic: ions; electrostatic forces; high mp; conducts only molten/aq.
  • Giant metallic: cations + delocalised e⁻; electrostatic forces; high mp; conducts solid & molten; malleable.
  • Giant covalent: atoms; covalent bonds; very high mp; usually non-conductor (graphite excepted).
  • Simple molecular: molecules; weak intermolecular (van der Waals) forces; low mp; non-conductor. Melting breaks the intermolecular forces, NOT the covalent bonds.

    □ Ionic definition missing "oppositely charged ions" / "electrostatic"

    □ Dot-and-cross ion without square brackets or charge

    □ Ionic strength explained by charge OR radius but not both

    □ Isoelectronic order reversed (forgot most protons = smallest)

    □ Polarising power (cation) confused with polarisability (anion)

    □ Covalent definition not stated as electrostatic attraction to the shared pair

    □ Dative arrow drawn the wrong way (must point donor → acceptor)

    □ Graphite conduction put "in the bonds" instead of "between the layers"

    □ Electronegativity defined without "the bonding pair in a covalent bond"

    □ CCl₄ called a polar molecule (polar bonds, but symmetric → non-polar)

    □ Lone-pair compression / repulsion order not quoted when explaining an angle
