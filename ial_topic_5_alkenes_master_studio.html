View: Complete C=C Double Bond (σ + π)

The C=C bond (612 kJ mol⁻¹, 0.134 nm) combines a strong axial σ bond (348 kJ mol⁻¹) and a weaker sideways π bond (~264 kJ mol⁻¹). Because the π electron density sits exposed above and below the plane, electrophiles attack the π bond while the σ framework remains intact.

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Alkenes

Electrophilic addition arrows

Curly arrow from C=C π bond → H of HX; arrow from H–X bond → X⁻; then lone pair → carbocation. Never from empty space.

Markovnikov

More stable carbocation forms: 3° > 2° > 1°. It is the tertiary CARBOCATION that is more stable — not the “tertiary halogenoalkane”.

E/Z isomerism

Needs BOTH restricted rotation about C=C AND two different groups on each C. “Different sides” alone is not enough.

σ vs π

σ = head-on overlap of orbitals (single bond); π = sideways overlap of p orbitals (second bond of C=C). Both parts of C=C are NOT π.

Br₂ test colours

Br₂(aq) orange/brown → colourless with alkene; stays brown with alkane. Cycloalkanes do NOT decolourise (no C=C).

Hydration reagent

Steam + H₃PO₄ catalyst (or conc. H₂SO₄ then water). Not NaOH/ethanol (that is elimination of halogenoalkanes).

Key Definitions
  • Alkene: an unsaturated hydrocarbon with at least one C=C double bond; general formula CnH2n (acyclic, one double bond).
  • Unsaturated: contains a C=C double bond (a σ bond + a π bond) — can undergo addition.
  • σ bond: head-on (axial) overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis — one region of overlap.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p-orbitals — electron density above and below the internuclear axis; weaker than σ; prevents rotation.
  • Electrophile: an electron-pair acceptor (electron-deficient species), e.g. H⁺, Br⁺ (in polarised Br₂).
  • Electrophilic addition: the characteristic alkene reaction — the π bond donates to an electrophile, then a nucleophile adds to the carbocation.
  • Carbocation: a positively charged carbon intermediate; stability 3° > 2° > 1°.
  • Geometric (E–Z) isomerism: stereoisomerism from restricted rotation about the C=C, with two different groups on each double-bond carbon.
  • Addition polymerisation: many alkene monomers join by opening their C=C π bonds to form one long saturated chain.

    Electrophilic Addition Mechanism (Spec 5.6)
  • Step 1: the π bond (high electron density) acts as a nucleophile and attacks the electrophile (δ⁺ atom). Curly arrow from the C=C bond to the electrophile; a second arrow shows the E–Nu bond breaking heterolytically onto the leaving group.
  • Intermediate: a carbocation forms on the other carbon.
  • Step 2: the nucleophile (anion, e.g. Br⁻) donates a lone pair to the carbocation. Curly arrow from the lone pair to C⁺.
  • Markovnikov: with an unsymmetrical alkene, H⁺ adds to the carbon with more H atoms, giving the more stable carbocation → major product. Stability: 3° > 2° > 1° (alkyl groups donate electron density, dispersing the + charge).
  • HBr + propene: major = 2-bromopropane (via 2° carbocation); minor = 1-bromopropane (via 1° carbocation).
    • E–Z Isomerism Rules (Spec 5.2 – 5.3)
  • Condition 1: restricted rotation about the C=C (the π bond locks the groups in place).
  • Condition 2: each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups.
  • Priority: on each carbon, rank the two attached groups by atomic number (higher = higher priority; Cahn–Ingold–Prelog).
  • Z (zusammen, "together"): the two high-priority groups are on the same side. E (entgegen, "opposite"): on opposite sides.
  • cis/trans works only when each C has a common group (usually H); with 3–4 different groups it is ambiguous → E/Z is needed.
  • No E/Z: chloroethene (CH₂=CHCl — one C has two H) and 2-methylpropene (one C has two CH₃).
    • Polymers (Spec 5.7 – 5.8)
  • Repeat unit: break the C=C π bond, draw the two carbons with one outward single bond each, wrap in brackets with n. The repeat unit has the same atoms as the monomer (addition polymerisation — no by-product).
  • ethene → poly(ethene) · propene → poly(propene) · chloroethene → poly(chloroethene) (PVC) · phenylethene → poly(phenylethene) (polystyrene) · methyl 2-methylpropenoate → Perspex.
  • Why inert: strong, non-polar C–C and C–H single bonds → unreactive → durable but non-biodegradable (landfill problem).
  • Disposal solutions: (i) develop biodegradable polymers (e.g. hydrolysable links); (ii) remove toxic gases from incineration — burning PVC releases HCl, scrubbed with an alkaline solution.
    • Suggested Practicals
  • Alkane vs alkene reactivity: alkenes decolourise bromine water and acidified KMnO₄ (alkanes do not, in the dark); alkenes burn with a smokier flame (higher C:H ratio).
  • Cyclohexene from cyclohexanol: acid-catalysed dehydration (conc. H₃PO₄, heat) — the reverse of hydration; distil off the cyclohexene.
  • Limonene from orange peel: steam distillation.
  • Perspex from methyl 2-methylpropenoate: addition polymerisation.
    • Master Trap Checklist

    ☐ Calling alkene + Br₂ a substitution — it is addition (no HBr by-product).

    ☐ Writing HBr + propene as only 1-bromopropane — the major product is 2-bromopropane.

    ☐ Curly arrows starting from an atom — they must start from a bond or lone pair.

    ☐ Forgetting the carbocation intermediate (and its charge).

    ☐ Saying rotation about C=C is "slow" — it is restricted / prevented by the π bond.

    ☐ Assigning E/Z by group size — priority is by atomic number.

    ☐ Using cis/trans for a molecule with 3–4 different groups — use E/Z.

    ☐ Drawing the repeat unit with a double bond — it must be a single C–C with outward bonds.

    ☐ Forgetting the brackets and n on the repeat unit.

    ☐ Saying polyalkenes "rot" — they are non-biodegradable; name the two disposal strategies.

    ☐ Hydration conditions — steam + conc. H₃PO₄, 300 °C, 60 atm (not dilute acid, r.t.).

    ☐ KMnO₄ — cold & dilute gives a diol; the purple colour decolourises.
