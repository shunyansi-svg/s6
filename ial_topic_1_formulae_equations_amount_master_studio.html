Spec 1.1 Terminology

Chemical Terminology — Atoms to Formulae

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Define atom (smallest particle of an element), element (one type of atom), ion (charged particle), molecule (two or more atoms covalently bonded), compound (two or more elements chemically combined)
  • Distinguish empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio of atoms) from molecular formula (actual number of each atom in a molecule)
  • Predict common ion charges from group number: Group I → +1, Group II → +2, Group VII → −1, O → −2
  • Recognise that ionic compounds (e.g. NaCl, MgO) use empirical formulae; covalent molecules (e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆) may have molecular formulae different from empirical
  • Identify polyatomic ions: OH⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO⁻, CO₃²⁻, NH₄⁺ — and use them correctly in formulae
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Empirical formula of glucose is CH₂O, not C₆H₁₂O₆ — confusing the two is a classic lost mark. An ion must carry a charge; a neutral atom is not an ion. "Compound" requires chemical bonding — a mixture is not a compound.

Spec 1.2 The Mole

The Mole & Avogadro Constant

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Define the mole as the amount of substance containing the same number of particles as exactly 12 g of carbon-12
  • State the Avogadro constant $L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ — note the unit mol⁻¹
  • Calculate number of particles: $N = n \times L$
  • Convert between mass, moles, and particles using $n = \frac{m}{M}$ and $N = nL$
  • Distinguish amount of substance (mol) from mass (g) — they measure different things
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

$L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ — the unit mol⁻¹ is a mark; writing just $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ loses it. "Number of moles" is acceptable; "moles" alone as the quantity name is not. Do not confuse $n$ (amount) with $N$ (number of particles).

Spec 1.3 Equations

Balanced Equations & State Symbols

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Write balanced full equations with correct stoichiometric coefficients — check atom counts on both sides
  • Include state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) for every species
  • Derive ionic equations: split aqueous strong electrolytes into ions, cancel spectator ions
  • Check both mass balance and charge balance in ionic equations
  • Recognise that weak acids (e.g. CH₃COOH) and insoluble solids are not split into ions
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Spectator ions left in the ionic equation = lost mark. State symbols required even when the question doesn't explicitly ask. CH₃COOH is a weak acid — write it as a molecule, not H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻. Charge balance: total charge on left must equal total charge on right.

Spec 1.4 Relative Masses

Relative Masses, Molar Mass & ppm

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals
  • Define $A_r$ (relative atomic mass) on the ¹²C scale: weighted mean mass of an atom relative to $\frac{1}{12}$ of ¹²C
  • Define $M_r$ (relative molecular mass) for covalent molecules; use relative formula mass for ionic / giant structures
  • Define molar mass as mass per mole in g mol⁻¹ (numerically equal to $A_r$ or $M_r$)
  • Calculate $A_r$ from isotopic data: $A_r = \frac{\sum(\text{isotope mass} \times \% \text{abundance})}{100}$
  • Define parts per million (ppm): $\text{ppm} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 10^6$
  • Apply ppm to atmospheric gases and dilute aqueous solutions
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Say "relative formula mass of NaCl" — not "relative molecular mass" (NaCl is ionic). Molar mass unit is g mol⁻¹; writing just "g" loses the mark. Isotopic $A_r$ calculations: use percentage or fractional abundance consistently — mixing them gives wrong answers.

Spec 1.5 Concentration

Concentration of Solutions

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Define concentration in mol dm⁻³ (molarity): $c = \frac{n}{V}$ where $V$ is in dm³
  • Define concentration in g dm⁻³: $c = \frac{m}{V}$ where $V$ is in dm³
  • Convert between mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³: $\text{g dm}^{-3} = \text{mol dm}^{-3} \times M$
  • Convert volumes: $1\ \text{dm}^3 = 1000\ \text{cm}^3$; divide cm³ by 1000 for dm³
  • Note: titration calculations are not required at this stage (deferred to Topic 3 / WCH12)
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

The #1 error: using cm³ directly in $c = n/V$ without converting to dm³. Write "mol dm⁻³" — not "mol/dm³" or "M" in IAL answers. If the question gives 250 cm³, you must write 0.250 dm³ in your working.

Spec 1.6 Formulae

Empirical & Molecular Formulae from Data

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Determine empirical formula from % composition or masses: convert each element to moles ($n = m/A_r$), divide by smallest, find simplest whole-number ratio
  • Determine molecular formula from empirical formula and $M_r$: multiply by $\frac{M_r}{\text{empirical mass}}$
  • Handle non-integer ratios: if ratio is 1 : 1.5, multiply all by 2 → 2 : 3 (do not round)
  • Use combustion data: mass of CO₂ → moles C; mass of H₂O → moles H; O by difference
  • Present working clearly in a table: element | mass | moles | ratio | empirical formula
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Divide by the smallest number of moles, not the largest. Ratio 1 : 1.33 → multiply by 3 (→ 3 : 4), not round to 1 : 1. Show the table — examiners award method marks even if arithmetic slips. O by difference: mass O = total mass − mass C − mass H.

Spec 1.7 Reacting Masses

Reacting Masses & Limiting Reagent

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Use balanced equation mole ratios to link reactant and product amounts
  • Calculate mass of product: $m \xrightarrow{\div M} n \xrightarrow{\text{ratio}} n \xrightarrow{\times M} m$
  • Identify the limiting reagent when amounts of both reactants are given — compare mole ratios
  • Calculate mass of excess reagent remaining after reaction
  • Work backwards: from mass of product, find mass of reactant needed
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Always identify the limiting reagent first — assuming the wrong one gives a completely wrong answer. The mole ratio comes from the balanced equation coefficients, not from the masses. "Reacting masses" means stoichiometric calculation, not just adding masses together.

Spec 1.8 Gas Volumes

Volumes of Gases & the Ideal Gas Equation

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals
  • Use molar volume at r.t.p.: $V_m = 24\ \text{dm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ (or 24 000 cm³ mol⁻¹)
  • Convert moles ↔ volume: $n = \frac{V}{24}$ (V in dm³) or $n = \frac{V}{24000}$ (V in cm³)
  • Apply $pV = nRT$ for non-standard conditions; $R = 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
  • Convert temperature: $T(\text{K}) = T(°\text{C}) + 273$
  • Ensure consistent SI units in $pV = nRT$: $p$ in Pa, $V$ in m³, $n$ in mol, $T$ in K
  • Use Avogadro's law: volume ratio = mole ratio for gases at same $T$ and $p$
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

In $pV = nRT$: pressure must be in Pa (not kPa or atm) and volume in m³ (not dm³ or cm³). Convert kPa → Pa (×1000); dm³ → m³ (÷1000). At r.t.p. use 24 dm³ mol⁻¹; at s.t.p. use 22.4 dm³ mol⁻¹ — do not mix them up.

Spec 1.9 Yield & Atom Economy

Percentage Yield & Atom Economy

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Calculate percentage yield: $\% \text{yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$
  • Calculate theoretical yield from stoichiometry using the limiting reagent
  • Calculate atom economy: $\% \text{atom economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product}}{\sum M_r \text{ of ALL products}} \times 100\%$
  • Explain why high atom economy matters: less waste, greener process, more sustainable
  • Distinguish yield (practical efficiency of a specific run) from atom economy (theoretical waste measure of the reaction itself)
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Atom economy denominator = all products, not reactants. A reaction can have 100% yield but poor atom economy (e.g. substitution). Addition reactions have 100% atom economy (one product). Do not confuse the two concepts — they measure different things.

Spec 1.10 Experimental Formulae

Determine Formula by Experiment

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals
  • Design or interpret experiments to find empirical formulae (e.g. heating a metal in oxygen, measuring mass change)
  • Use mass data before and after reaction to find mass of each element, then convert to moles
  • Evaluate experimental data: identify sources of error (incomplete reaction, product loss, moisture absorption)
  • Confirm a balanced equation by comparing experimental mole ratios with theoretical coefficients
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

"Evaluate the data" = discuss reliability and suggest improvements — not just "the results are inaccurate". Common errors: incomplete combustion, product lost as smoke/gas, crucible not dried before first weighing, lid not replaced during heating.

Spec 1.11 Core Practical 1

CP1 — Measurement of Molar Volume of a Gas

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Describe the method: react known mass of metal (e.g. Mg ribbon) with excess dilute acid; collect gas in a gas syringe or by water displacement
  • Calculate moles of gas from measured volume: $n = \frac{V}{24}$ (at r.t.p.) or use $pV = nRT$
  • Compare experimental molar volume with accepted value (24 dm³ mol⁻¹ at r.t.p.); calculate % error
  • Identify systematic errors: gas escaping before bung inserted, gas dissolving in water, $T$/$p$ not at r.t.p.
  • State the direction of each error's effect on the calculated molar volume
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Gas dissolving in water → lower measured volume → lower molar volume. Gas escaping before bung sealed → also lower volume. You must state the direction of the error, not just "there is an error". Excess acid ensures all metal reacts — this is a control, not an error source.

Spec 1.12 Ionic from Observations

Ionic & Full Equations from Test-Tube Observations

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals
  • Write full and ionic equations for displacement reactions: more reactive metal displaces less reactive (e.g. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu)
  • Write full and ionic equations for acid reactions: acid + metal → salt + H₂; acid + base → salt + H₂O; acid + carbonate → salt + H₂O + CO₂
  • Write full and ionic equations for precipitation reactions: two soluble salts → insoluble precipitate + soluble salt
  • Relate observations to species: colour change (transition metal ions), effervescence (gas = CO₂ or H₂), precipitate (insoluble solid)
  • Include state symbols and cancel spectator ions in every ionic equation
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs

Displacement: check the reactivity series — a metal cannot displace one above it. Precipitation ionic equations show only the ions forming the precipitate (e.g. Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)). Effervescence with acid + carbonate = CO₂, not O₂. H₂ from acid + metal is colourless and odourless — "pops" with a lit splint.

Out of scope in this studio: Titration calculations (deferred to Topic 3 / WCH12); redox equations and oxidation numbers (Topic 2 / WCH11 §2); enthalpy changes (Topic 8 / WCH13). These build on Topic 1 foundations but are taught in later units.

Equation Type Decision Tree

Start: What type of reaction?

├─ Metal + acid? → salt + H₂(g) — ionic: metal + 2H⁺ → metal²⁺ + H₂

├─ Acid + base/alkali? → salt + H₂O — ionic: H⁺ + OH → H₂O

├─ Acid + carbonate? → salt + H₂O + CO₂(g) — ionic: 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → H₂O + CO₂

├─ Metal + salt solution? → displacement if metal is more reactive

├─ Two solutions mixed? → check solubility rules → precipitate if insoluble product forms

└─ Combustion? → substance + O₂ → oxides (usually CO₂ + H₂O for hydrocarbons)

💡 Solubility Quick Rules

All nitrates soluble. All Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺ salts soluble. Most chlorides soluble (except AgCl, PbCl₂). Most sulfates soluble (except BaSO₄, PbSO₄, CaSO₄). Most carbonates insoluble (except Na₂CO₃, K₂CO₃). Most hydroxides insoluble (except NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂).

Concept Cards

Formula Determination Flowchart

Given % or mass data:

1. List each element with its mass (or % as mass in 100 g)

2. Convert each to moles: $n = m / A_r$

3. Divide all by the smallest mole value

4. If ratios are not whole numbers, multiply all by 2, 3, … until they are

5. Write empirical formula from the whole-number ratio

If Mr is also given:

6. Calculate empirical mass; $n = M_r / \text{empirical mass}$

7. Multiply empirical formula subscripts by $n$ → molecular formula
