Topic 1 Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Spec 1.1 – 1.12)

Review essential learning goals and examiner watch-outs across the full in-scope unit — formulae, equations and amounts of substance.

Spec 1.1 Core vocabulary

Atoms, elements, ions, molecules, compounds, empirical & molecular formulae

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define atom as the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction.
  • Define element as a substance made of atoms of only one type — it cannot be broken down chemically into simpler substances.
  • Define molecule as two or more atoms bonded together: same element (H₂, O₂) = molecule of an element; different elements (H₂O) = molecule of a compound.
  • Define ion as a charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻).
  • Define compound as a substance containing atoms of different elements chemically combined in fixed ratios.
  • Distinguish empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio) from molecular formula (actual number of atoms in one molecule).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A molecule of an element contains atoms of the same element (O₂); a molecule of a compound contains atoms of different elements (H₂O) — mixing these loses the definition mark. For ionic compounds (giant lattices) use “relative formula mass”, never “molecular mass”. Glacial wording: “relative formula mass is preferred for ionic compounds because they exist as giant ionic lattices rather than discrete molecules”.

Spec 1.2 The mole & Avogadro

The mole (mol) and the Avogadro constant L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State that the mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of particles as atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
  • State the Avogadro constant L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ — the number of particles in one mole.
  • Convert between amount and particle number: $n = \dfrac{N}{L}$.
  • Count all particles a formula unit provides — e.g. 1 mol Fe₂(SO₄)₃ contains 5 mol ions (2 Fe³⁺ + 3 SO₄²⁻); 1 mol Mohr's salt (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O contains 5 mol ions (2 NH₄⁺ + Fe²⁺ + 2 SO₄²⁻).
  • Use $N = nL$ for atoms, molecules, ions and electrons (e.g. electrons transferred in electrolysis).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The definition must say the same number of particles as atoms in 12 g of carbon-12 — not “12 g of carbon”. When counting ions, include every ion in the formula unit: 2 mol of Mohr's salt gives 6 mol cations (4 NH₄⁺ + 2 Fe²⁺) → 3.61 × 10²⁴ particles (Jan 2026 Q24a(ii)) — students count only Fe²⁺. Multiply by L, never divide.

Spec 1.3 Equations

Balanced full and ionic equations, including state symbols

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Write balanced full equations with state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) — balance atoms and charge.
  • Split soluble ionic compounds into ions to build the ionic equation; cancel the spectator ions.
  • Example: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq); ionic: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) — NO₃⁻ and Na⁺ are spectators.
  • Balance ionic equations for charge as well as atoms (e.g. Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂).
  • Write equations for displacement, acid–base and precipitation reactions (Spec 1.12).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never change subscripts to balance — coefficients only. IAL accepts multiples/fractions (½O₂ allowed) but a wrong 2:1 ratio caps downstream marks. State symbols are usually ignored unless (aq)/(s) matters for an observation — but write them anyway. Net ionic equation must show the reacting species only: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

Spec 1.4 Relative masses

Aᵣ, Mᵣ, relative formula mass and molar mass (¹²C scale)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) as the weighted mean mass of an atom compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom — unitless.
  • Define relative molecular mass (Mᵣ) as the sum of Aᵣ of all atoms in a molecule (molecular substances only).
  • Use relative formula mass for ionic / giant structures (e.g. NaCl, CuSO₄·5H₂O) — never “molecular mass”.
  • Define molar mass (M) as mass per mole, units g mol⁻¹, numerically equal to Aᵣ or Mᵣ.
  • Calculate Aᵣ from isotopic abundances: Aᵣ = Σ(isotope mass × abundance) ÷ 100 (e.g. chlorine 35.49 from 75.52% ³⁵Cl + 24.48% ³⁷Cl).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Aᵣ definition needs the word atom and the ¹²C = 12 comparison — reject “element/isotopes”. Relative masses are unitless; molar mass carries g mol⁻¹. Aᵣ of chlorine = 35.49 (weighted mean, not 35.5 straight average) — unweighted averaging loses the mark. Penalise units other than g mol⁻¹ when a mass is requested.

Spec 1.5 Solutions

Concentration in mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Use $c = \dfrac{n}{V}$ with V in dm³ — convert cm³ → dm³ by ÷ 1000.
  • Convert between mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³: g dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³ × Mᵣ.
  • Work moles from concentration and volume: $n = cV$ (test-tube scale: 22.5 cm³ of 1.6 mol dm⁻³ HCl → 0.036 mol).
  • Dilution: $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$ (titration-style scaling, ×10/×20/×40).
  • Multi-step: mass → moles → concentration for a stated volume (e.g. 0.13 g AgBr in 400 cm³ → 1.73 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The classic kill: leaving volume in cm³ — ÷ 1000 first. Wrong units (or missing mol dm⁻³) lose the final mark. Count every atom in Mᵣ (e.g. 2 Cl in CaCl₂). Titration proper is not required at this stage, but test-tube concentration + dilution calculations are fair game. Concordant titres within 0.2 cm³; burette % error = (0.05 × 100) ÷ titre.

Spec 1.6 Formulae from data

Empirical and molecular formulae from experimental data

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Route 1 — % composition: assume 100 g → mass ÷ Aᵣ → moles → divide by smallest → whole-number ratio.
  • Route 2 — combustion data: mass CO₂ → moles C; mass H₂O → moles H (×2); O by subtraction if present.
  • Molecular formula = empirical × n, where $n = \dfrac{M_r}{\text{empirical } M_r}$ (e.g. CH₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ via Mᵣ 180).
  • Fractional ratios: 1 : 1.5 → ×2 → 2 : 3; 1 : 1.25 → ×4 (never round 1.5 → 1).
  • Use Mᵣ evidence (mass spec molecular ion peak, ideal gas experiment) to convert empirical → molecular.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Using atomic numbers instead of masses gets no transferred error. Not dividing by the smallest mole value, or rounding 1.5 → 1, is penalised. Molecular formula without Mᵣ evidence scores empirical credit only. In combustion analysis H₂O supplies 2 H per molecule — forgetting this halves the H ratio. CeO₂-style MC (Oct 2025 Q11): ratio from masses, not charges.

Spec 1.7 Reacting masses

Reacting masses from balanced equations (and vice versa)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Workflow: mass → moles ($n = m/M$) → mole ratio from balanced equation → moles of target → mass ($m = nM$).
  • Identify the limiting reagent when both reactant masses are given — the one that runs out first.
  • Percentage purity: $\text{purity} = \dfrac{\text{mass of pure}}{\text{mass of sample}} \times 100$.
  • Scale by solution volume when only part of a solution reacts (e.g. moles for 25 cm³ of a 250 cm³ solution).
  • Worked: Mohr's salt — n(FeSO₄) = 6.95/277.9 → ×392 → ×78.2/100 → 7.67 g (Jan 2025/Oct 2025 pattern).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Transferred error (TE) is allowed from a wrong mass or Mᵣ — but a wrong mole ratio caps downstream marks. % yield over 100% ⇒ check the limiting reagent (Oct 2025 Q17e: CuSO₄ limiting, yield 96.0%). Ignore SF except 1 SF; penalise units once only. “If Mᵣ incorrect can only score M₁.”

Spec 1.8 Gas volumes

Volumes of gases: amount of substance and molar volume

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State the molar volume: 1 mol of any gas occupies 24.0 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.).
  • Convert moles ↔ volume: $n = \dfrac{V}{24}$ (dm³) or $n = \dfrac{V}{24000}$ (cm³).
  • Gas stoichiometry: moles from equation → volume × 24 (e.g. pentane combustion → 15 650 cm³ CO₂).
  • Include all gaseous species and leftover excess gas when calculating total gas volumes.
  • Ideal gas equation for non-r.t.p. conditions: $pV = nRT$ — p in Pa, V in m³, T in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

pV = nRT demands Kelvin first (K = °C + 273), V in m³ (dm³ ÷ 1000), p in Pa — mixing kPa/Pa or cm³/m³ costs the unit mark (value 0.899 vs 0.899 dm³: 1-mark difference). Molar volume 24 000 cm³ must match ×24 000, not ×24. Q22c(ii)-style: 430 cm³ ÷ 24 000 = 0.0179 mol — rounding to 0.017 (2 SF) is penalised.

Spec 1.9 Yield & atom economy

Percentage yield and percentage atom economy by mass

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • $\text{Percentage yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$.
  • State reasons for yield < 100%: incomplete reaction (reversible), side reactions, loss during purification/transfer, experimental errors.
  • $\text{Atom economy} = \dfrac{M_r(\text{desired product})}{\Sigma M_r(\text{all products})} \times 100$ — by mass or moles of Mᵣ.
  • Addition reactions have 100% atom economy (one product); elimination/substitution reactions are lower.
  • High atom economy ⇒ less waste; worked: 40.3/84.3 × 100 = 47.8% vs 40.3/58.3 × 100 = 69.1%.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Swapping actual/theoretical or ×10 errors score 0 — “4.24% scores (0)”. For low yield, reject bare “reaction reversible” or “incomplete reaction”; allow “loss of material on transferring / equilibrium losses / practical losses”. Atom economy: reject “no waste” alone. Both yield and atom economy need the ×100 to be visible.

Spec 1.10 Formula by experiment

Determining a formula or confirming an equation by experiment

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Water of crystallisation: heat hydrated salt to constant mass → mass water lost → mol H₂O : mol anhydrous → x in formula.
  • Formula of an oxide by reduction: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O; masses before/after give the Cu : O ratio.
  • Confirm an equation from gas or precipitate data (lithium + water, magnesium + acid).
  • Continuous variation: keep one reactant fixed, vary the other, find the ratio giving maximum precipitate — the stoichiometric ratio.
  • Evaluate data: incomplete reaction, loss during purification, impurities, side reactions, gas solubility (CO₂ cannot be collected over water).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Heat to constant mass” means heat → weigh → repeat until no further mass change — “repeat three times” alone is not the reason. CO₂ must not be collected over water (measurable solubility) ⇒ underestimates the volume. In % O by subtraction, missing the oxygen mass scores M2 only. Gravimetric analysis = measuring mass changes.

Spec 1.11 CORE PRACTICAL 1

CP1 — Measurement of the molar volume of a gas

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • React a known mass of metal (e.g. Mg) with excess acid and collect the H₂ gas in a gas syringe (or over water if insoluble).
  • Measure the volume of gas at room temperature and pressure; record T and p.
  • Moles of gas from the equation (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂, 1 : 1); molar volume = V ÷ n.
  • Compare with the accepted 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at r.t.p.; calculate percentage error.
  • Improve accuracy: excess acid (ensures all Mg reacts), cool apparatus to r.t.p., repeat and average.
  • Link to the WCH13 paper: same gas maths tested as method + error analysis — practise in Tab 8.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Gas syringe needs the syringe to be airtight; H₂ is insoluble in water so collection over water is fine — but never for CO₂. Measure the volume after the apparatus cools to r.t.p. (hot gas expands). % error = (experimental − true)/true × 100; smaller titre/volume = larger % error. Percentage error = (absolute error ÷ measured value) × 100%.

Spec 1.12 Test-tube reactions

Ionic & full equations ↔ observations: displacement, acids, precipitation

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Displacement: more reactive element displaces the less reactive one (Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu; blue colour fades, brown/pink solid forms).
  • Acids: acid + metal → salt + H₂; acid + carbonate → salt + CO₂ + H₂O (effervescence); acid + base/oxide → salt + H₂O (neutralisation).
  • Precipitation: mixing solutions forms an insoluble solid — Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq), yellow precipitate.
  • Match observation ↔ ionic equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) (white precipitate); Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).
  • Predict products from the ion pairs present; spectator ions stay in solution.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Colour must be right: Cu²⁺(aq) is blue; PbI₂ is yellow; AgCl is white. Displacement needs the reactivity direction stated — zinc displaces copper because Zn is more reactive. In continuous-variation graphs, maximum precipitate = stoichiometric ratio (Pb(NO₃)₂ : KI = 1 : 2). Write the ionic equation with charges, not just symbols.

Core Definitions & Formulae Inspector

Spec 1.1, 1.2, 1.4 — the vocabulary and the one-line equations that carry every mark.

Pick a term

1.2 · 1.4

Mole (mol)

$$n = \frac{N}{L} \qquad n = \frac{m}{M}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • “Amount of substance that contains the same number of particles as the number of carbon atoms in exactly 12 g of ¹²C.”
  • Unit: mol. Converts mass→moles ($n = m/M$), volume→moles (gas, $n = V/24$), concentration→moles ($n = cV$).
  • 1 mol of any substance contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Must say “12 g of carbon-12” — not “12 g of carbon”.
  • “Particles” = atoms, molecules, ions, electrons — count them all.
1.2

Avogadro constant (L)

$$L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • The number of particles in exactly one mole of any substance.
  • Ties the mole to a countable number of particles — bridges the microscopic and macroscopic worlds.
  • Use $N = nL$ to find particle counts; $n = N/L$ to find amount.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Unit is mol⁻¹ — never forget it in the answer.
  • Multiply by L to count ions in a formula unit (e.g. 2 mol Mohr's salt → 6 mol cations).
1.4

Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ)

$$A_r = \frac{\text{weighted mean mass of one atom}}{\tfrac{1}{12}\ \text{mass of one }^{12}\text{C atom}}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Weighted mean mass of an atom, compared with 1/12 of the mass of a ¹²C atom.
  • Unitless — it is a ratio on the ¹²C scale.
  • Calculate from isotopic abundance: Aᵣ = Σ(mass × abundance) ÷ 100.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Weighted mean” — a plain average (35 + 37)/2 = 36 loses the mark.
  • Must mention “atom” and “¹²C = 12” for the definition mark.
1.4

Relative molecular mass (Mᵣ)

$$M_r = \sum (\text{A}_r \times \text{number of atoms})$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule.
  • Used for molecular substances (covalent): H₂O = 18.0, CO₂ = 44.0.
  • Unitless — relative mass on the ¹²C scale.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Count every atom — e.g. CaCl₂ Mᵣ = 40.1 + 2×35.5 = 111.1 (not 75.6).
  • Never use “molecular mass” for ionic compounds.
1.4

Relative formula mass

$$M_r(\text{NaCl}) = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Same sum as Mᵣ but used for giant structures — ionic lattices (NaCl) and hydrates (CuSO₄·5H₂O).
  • Preferred because ionic compounds have no discrete molecules.
  • Unitless.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Molecular mass implies discrete molecules — wrong for giant ionic lattices.”
  • Include water of crystallisation in the mass: CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.7.
1.4

Molar mass (M)

$$M = \frac{m}{n} \qquad \text{units: g mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mass of one mole of a substance — numerically equal to Aᵣ or Mᵣ.
  • Units g mol⁻¹ (the only mass in this topic that carries units).
  • Central converter: $n = m/M$, $m = nM$, $M = m/n$.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Confusing relative mass (unitless) with molar mass (g mol⁻¹) is a top classic error.
  • Mᵣ from ideal gas: Mᵣ = mRT / pV.
1.1 · 1.6

Empirical formula

$$\text{CH}_2\text{O}\ \text{(glucose)} \qquad \text{simplest whole-number ratio}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Smallest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
  • From % composition: mass ÷ Aᵣ → moles → ratio ÷ smallest → simplify.
  • Ionic compounds are always expressed as empirical formulae.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Divide by the smallest moles — skipping this is a classic error.
  • 1.5 × 2 → 3; never round 1.5 → 1.
1.1 · 1.6

Molecular formula

$$C_6H_{12}O_6 = (CH_2O)_6 \qquad n = \frac{M_r}{M_r(\text{empirical})}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
  • n = Mᵣ ÷ empirical Mᵣ (e.g. 180 ÷ 30 = 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆).
  • Needs Mᵣ evidence (mass spec molecular ion, ideal gas experiment).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • No Mᵣ evidence ⇒ empirical credit only.
  • Wrong m/e read ⇒ no TE on the molecular formula.
1.8

Molar volume

$$V_m = 24.0\ \text{dm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ (24\,000\ \text{cm}^3)\ \text{at r.t.p.}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Volume occupied by 1 mol of any gas at room temperature and pressure.
  • r.t.p. ≈ 25 °C, 100 kPa (1 atm).
  • Convert: $n = V/24$ (dm³) or $n = V/24\,000$ (cm³).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Match the unit: dm³ ↔ ×24; cm³ ↔ ×24 000. Mismatch penalised once.
  • Only for gases — liquids/solids have their own densities.
1.5

Concentration

$$c = \frac{n}{V}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} \qquad \text{g dm}^{-3} = \text{mol dm}^{-3} \times M_r$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Amount of solute per unit volume of solution.
  • V must be in dm³ — cm³ ÷ 1000.
  • Convert mass↔moles via Mᵣ to get g dm⁻³.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Leaving volume in cm³ is the #1 killer.
  • mol dm⁻³ required — wrong units lose the last mark.
1.3 · 1.12

Spectator ion

$$\text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)} + \text{NaNO}_3\text{(aq)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Ion present before and after the reaction — not involved in it.
  • Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators here; cancel them → Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • Ionic equation shows reacting species only.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Spectator ions must not appear in the ionic equation.
  • Charge balance: 2Ag⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → 2AgCl(s) simplifies to 1:1.
1.10

Hydrate / water of crystallisation

$$\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad M_r = 249.7$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Compound with water of crystallisation trapped in the lattice.
  • Find x: heat to constant mass → mass water lost → mol H₂O : mol anhydrous salt.
  • Include the water in Mᵣ: CuSO₄·5H₂O = 159.6 + 90.1 = 249.7.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Heat to constant mass” = repeat until no mass change — not “heat three times”.
  • Forgetting the ·5H₂O in Mᵣ ruins the whole calculation.

The Mole Highway — every road starts at n

All Topic 1 calculations are routes into or out of the mole. Master the four gates and every question becomes a journey.

Gate 1 · Mass

$n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ — grams ÷ molar mass (g mol⁻¹). The most-used gate; needs Mᵣ from the periodic table.

Gate 2 · Particles

$n = \dfrac{N}{L}$ — count ÷ Avogadro. Remember to count every ion in a formula unit.

Gate 3 · Gas volume

$n = \dfrac{V}{24}$ (dm³) or $n = \dfrac{V}{24\,000}$ (cm³) at r.t.p.; $pV = nRT$ for other conditions.

Gate 4 · Solution

$n = cV$ (V in dm³) — concentration × volume. Convert g dm⁻³ ↔ mol dm⁻³ via Mᵣ.

Every calculation is a route

Mass → Gate 1 → n → mole ratio (balanced equation) → n of target → Gate 1/3/4 → answer. Reacting masses, gas volumes from equations, concentrations — all the same highway.

Multi-blank Quiz — Definitions

1. The mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of particles as atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
2. The Avogadro constant L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ particles per mole.
3. Relative atomic mass is the weighted mean mass of an atom compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
4. Relative formula mass is used for ionic compounds / giant structures because they have no discrete molecules.
5. Molar mass has units g mol⁻¹ and is numerically equal to Aᵣ or Mᵣ.
6. The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound; the molecular formula is the actual number of atoms in one molecule.
7. At r.t.p., one mole of any gas occupies 24.0 dm³ (or 24 000 cm³).
8. In AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃, the spectator ions are Na⁺ and NO₃⁻.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Definitions

Relative mass vs Molar mass

Aᵣ / Mᵣ are unitless ratios on the ¹²C scale. Molar mass M carries g mol⁻¹. Never write “Mᵣ = 18 g mol⁻¹” — that is M.

Molecule vs Compound

A molecule of an element = same atoms (O₂). A molecule of a compound = different atoms (H₂O). O₂ is NOT a compound — it is a molecule of an element.

Empirical vs Molecular

CH₂O (empirical, simplest ratio) vs C₆H₁₂O₆ (molecular, actual). Two different compounds can share one empirical formula (CH₂ = ethene and cycloalkanes).

Molecular mass vs Formula mass

“Molecular mass” implies discrete molecules — wrong for giant ionic lattices. Use “relative formula mass” for NaCl, CuSO₄·5H₂O, (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Aᵣ weighted vs plain average

Chlorine Aᵣ = 35.49 (weighted by abundance 75.52/24.48), not (35+37)/2 = 36. Weighting is the whole point — isotopes are not equally abundant.

Moles → particles direction

n = N/L (divide by L) vs N = nL (multiply by L). 2 mol of Mohr's salt → 2 × 5 × 6.02 × 10²³ ions — multiply, never divide.

1.3 · 1.12

Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

$$\text{Mg} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Metal (above H in reactivity) + acid → salt + H₂ gas.
  • Ionic: Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂ — the chloride is a spectator.
  • Observation: effervescence (bubbles of H₂); metal dissolves.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • M must be 2+ here (MgCl₂) — charge determines the formula.
  • State symbols: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
1.3 · 1.12

Acid + carbonate → salt + CO₂ + water

$$\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Carbonate + acid → salt + carbon dioxide + water.
  • Ionic: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Test for the gas: limewater turns milky/cloudy.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CO₂ is soluble in water — collect over gas syringe, not over water.
  • 2H⁺ needed for CO₃²⁻ — charge balance.
1.3 · 1.12

Acid + base/oxide → salt + water

$$\text{CuO} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Insoluble metal oxide (base) + acid → salt + water.
  • Observation: black CuO dissolves → blue solution (Cu²⁺(aq)).
  • Ionic: O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O (a common ionic equation).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • CuO is insoluble — it stays (s); the ionic equation is O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O.
  • Blue solution = hydrated Cu²⁺ — the #1 observation to quote.
1.3 · 1.12

Displacement: Zn + Cu²⁺

$$\text{Zn(s)} + \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • More reactive metal displaces the less reactive one from its salt.
  • Ionic: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) — SO₄²⁻ spectator.
  • Observation: blue CuSO₄ fades/discolours; brown/pink copper solid deposits.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Full equation: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
  • Reactivity direction must be stated: Zn is more reactive than Cu.
1.3 · 1.12

Precipitation: AgCl & BaSO₄

🎯 Learning Goals

$$\text{NaOH(aq)} + \text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{NaCl(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$
🎯 Learning Goals

💡 Examiner Tips

  • Balance C first, then H, then O (O₂ coefficient last).

Reaction Map — test-tube chemistry

Every observation you can be asked to interpret, and the ions doing the work.

Metal + acid

Effervescence (H₂); metal dissolves. Ionic: M + 2H⁺ → M²⁺ + H₂. Only metals above hydrogen react.

Carbonate + acid

Effervescence (CO₂); limewater milky. Ionic: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.

Oxide + acid

Solid dissolves; CuO (black) → Cu²⁺ (blue). Ionic: O²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O.

Displacement

More reactive metal replaces less reactive; solution colour fades, solid deposits. Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu.

Precipitation

Insoluble solid forms on mixing. AgCl white, BaSO₄ white, PbI₂ yellow. Ionic: reacting ions only.

Neutralisation

Acid + alkali → salt + water. Ionic: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. pH moves toward 7.

Multi-blank Quiz — Equations

1. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Ionic: Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂.
2. CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O. The test for CO₂ is limewater turns milky/cloudy.
3. CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O. Observation: black solid dissolves to give a blue solution.
4. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. The blue colour fades/discolours.
5. Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) — a white precipitate.
6. The universal neutralisation ionic equation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
7. Complete combustion of C₃H₈: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.
8. In Core Practical 1, H₂ is collected in a gas syringe; the acid must be in excess so all Mg reacts.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Equations

Full equation vs Ionic equation

Full: all formulae with state symbols. Ionic: only reacting species, spectator ions cancelled. AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃; ionic = Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl.

Coefficient vs Subscript

Balance with coefficients (2MgO), never subscripts (MgO₂ is a different compound). Atoms are conserved; formulas are sacred.

H₂ vs 2H vs 2H⁺

H₂ = hydrogen molecule; 2H = two separate atoms; 2H⁺ = two hydrogen ions. In equations, H₂ is the gas; H⁺ is the aqueous ion.

CO₂ collection

CO₂ is soluble → collect with a gas syringe, not over water. H₂ is insoluble → collection over water is fine. This is a classic CP1 trap.

White precipitate chaos

AgCl (white), BaSO₄ (white), CaCO₃ (white) — learn the confirmatory tests. PbI₂ is the yellow one; Cu(OH)₂ is blue.

Reactivity direction

Zn displaces Cu (Zn more reactive). Cu can never displace Zn from ZnSO₄. State the direction — “it displaces because it is more reactive”.

1.4 · 1.7

Mᵣ / Aᵣ + reacting masses

$$n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad \text{mass} \rightarrow \text{moles} \rightarrow \text{mole ratio} \rightarrow \text{moles} \rightarrow \text{mass}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Convert mass → moles ($n = m/M$) and back ($m = nM$).
  • Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation (A + 2B → C: 2× moles of A = moles of B).
  • Worked: 6.95 g FeSO₄ → n = 6.95/277.9 → ×392 → ×78.2/100 = 7.67 g Mohr's salt.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • TE from wrong Mr allowed — but a wrong mole ratio caps downstream marks.
  • “If Mr incorrect can only score M₁.”
1.6

Empirical formula from % composition

$$40.0\%\ \text{C} : 6.7\%\ \text{H} : 53.3\%\ \text{O} \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Assume 100 g → % become grams → ÷ Aᵣ → moles → ÷ smallest → ratio.
  • Worked: C 40/12 = 3.33; H 6.7/1 = 6.7; O 53.3/16 = 3.33 → 1 : 2 : 1 → CH₂O.
  • Whole numbers only — multiply fractional ratios (1 : 1.5 → 2 : 3).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Using atomic numbers instead of masses → no TE.
  • Not dividing by the smallest = classic lost mark.
1.6

Combustion data → formula

$$\text{m CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{mol C} \qquad \text{m H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{mol H} \ (\times 2)$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Mass CO₂ → moles C (÷ 44); mass H₂O → moles H (÷ 18, then ×2 for the two H).
  • If O present: m O = total mass − (m C + m H); ÷ 16 → moles O.
  • Ratio C : H : O → empirical formula.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Forgetting ×2 for H₂O halves the H ratio — the classic error.
  • Missing O mass → “% of O missing → award M2 only”.
1.6

Molecular formula via Mᵣ

$$n = \frac{M_r}{M_r(\text{empirical})} \qquad \text{CH}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{M_r = 180} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • n = Mᵣ ÷ empirical Mᵣ; molecular = empirical × n (must be a whole number).
  • Mᵣ evidence: mass spec molecular ion peak, or ideal-gas experiment (Mᵣ = mRT/pV).
  • Example: empirical CH₂ (Mᵣ 14), molecular Mᵣ 70 → n = 5 → C₅H₁₀.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • No Mᵣ evidence ⇒ empirical credit only.
  • Wrong m/e molecular ion read ⇒ no TE on the formula.
1.7

Limiting reagent

$$n(\text{(NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4) = 0.0254 \qquad n(\text{CuSO}_4) = 0.0248 \rightarrow \text{CuSO}_4\ \text{limiting}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Convert both reactant masses to moles; compare with the stoichiometric ratio.
  • The reagent that runs out first is limiting; the other is in excess.
  • Worked (Oct 2025 Q17e): CuSO₄ limiting → double-salt yield 96.0%.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • % yield > 100% ⇒ check the limiting reagent — you used the excess.
  • “Reacting ratio = 2:1 and … less than double” is the classic MS phrasing.
1.10

Water of crystallisation

$$\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CuSO}_4 + x\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Heat to constant mass → mass of water lost = initial − final.
  • mol H₂O = m/18; mol anhydrous = m/Mᵣ(anhydrous); x = ratio.
  • Worked: 0.100 g hydrate → 0.064 g anhydrous (0.036 g H₂O) → x = 5 (CuSO₄·5H₂O).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • “Heat to constant mass” = repeat until no further mass change.
  • Include the water in Mᵣ when asked for the hydrate's formula mass.
1.9

Percentage yield

$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100 \qquad \frac{12.52}{18.475} \times 100 = 67.8\%$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Theoretical = maximum possible mass (complete reaction, no losses).
  • Actual = mass obtained in the experiment.
  • Reasons < 100%: incomplete/reversible reaction, side reactions, loss on transfer/purification, experimental error.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Bare “reaction reversible” / “incomplete reaction” is rejected — say “equilibrium losses / practical losses / loss on transfer”.
  • Yield > 100% ⇒ limiting-reagent error.
1.9

Atom economy

$$\text{AE} = \frac{M_r(\text{desired})}{\Sigma M_r(\text{all products})} \times 100 \qquad \frac{40.3}{84.3} \times 100 = 47.8\%$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Fraction of reactant atoms ending in the desired product, by mass.
  • Addition reactions → 100% atom economy (one product).
  • High AE = less waste, greener process.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Reject “no waste” on its own as a definition.
  • Allow mass or Mᵣ of desired × 100 ÷ total mass or Mᵣ of reactants/products.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Moles

Atomic number vs Atomic mass

Empirical/formula calcs use masses (Aᵣ), never atomic numbers (Z). Using Z scores no TE — e.g. C as 6 instead of 12.0 ruins the ratio.

Divide vs Multiply by L

n = N/L (particles → moles: divide). N = nL (moles → particles: multiply). Count ALL ions in the formula unit first (2 mol Mohr's salt → 6 mol cations).

Rounding 1.5 → 1

Ratio 1 : 1.5 is NOT 1 : 1 — multiply through by 2 → 2 : 3. Rounding to integers early loses the mark. 1.33 : 1 → ×3; 1.25 → ×4.

H₂O supplies 2 H

In combustion analysis, mol H = 2 × mol H₂O. Forgetting the factor of 2 halves the H ratio and gives the wrong empirical formula.

Yield > 100%

Impossible in theory — you used the wrong (excess) reagent as limiting, or the product is wet/impure. Recheck the limiting reagent.

“Reversible” ≠ valid reason

For low yield, bare “reaction is reversible” or “incomplete” is rejected. Accept “equilibrium losses / practical losses / loss of material on transferring / side reactions”.

1.8

Molar volume at r.t.p.

$$V_m = 24.0\ \text{dm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1} = 24\,000\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • 1 mol of ANY gas occupies 24.0 dm³ at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.).
  • r.t.p. ≈ 25 °C, 100 kPa (1 atm).
  • $n = V/24$ (dm³) or $n = V/24\,000$ (cm³).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Unit match: dm³ × 24; cm³ × 24 000. Mismatch (×24 with cm³) penalised once.
  • Only for gases — not liquids or solids.
1.8

Gas stoichiometry

$$\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12} + 8\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 5\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Moles from equation → volume via × 24 (dm³) or × 24 000 (cm³).
  • Worked (Oct 2025 Q21c): 15 g pentane → n = 15×0.626/72… → V(CO₂) = 15 650 cm³.
  • Include ALL gaseous species and leftover excess gas for total volume.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Jan 2026 Q12: forgetting leftover O₂ or the LHS volume loss loses the “total gas” mark.
  • TE each stage; ignore SF except 1.
1.8

Ideal gas equation pV = nRT

$$pV = nRT \qquad V = \frac{nRT}{p}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • p in Pa, V in m³, n in mol, T in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • Convert: dm³ → m³ ÷ 1000; cm³ → m³ ÷ 10⁶; °C → K + 273.
  • Worked (Oct 2025 Q15a): 5.998 m³ — three-stage TE chain.
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Kelvin first: K = °C + 273. Using °C directly is the top trap (Jan 2026 Q13).
  • Watch kPa vs Pa: 110 kPa = 1.10 × 10⁵ Pa.
1.8

Mᵣ from ideal gas

$$M_r = \frac{mRT}{pV}$$
🎯 Learning Goals
  • Rearrange pV = nRT with n = m/Mᵣ → Mᵣ = mRT/pV.
  • Works for volatile liquids too (vaporise a known mass into a gas syringe).
  • Feeds molecular-formula questions (empirical → Mᵣ → molecular).
💡 Examiner Tips
  • Mass must be in g, V in m³, T in K — one wrong unit and Mᵣ is off by factor 1000.
  • ECF allowed for wrong T in this equation.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Gases

dm³ vs m³ in pV = nRT

V in m³ only. 0.48 dm³ → 0.00048 m³. Using dm³ gives V 1000× too big. Check the answer magnitude — 5.998 m³ ≈ 6000 dm³.

°C vs K

T must be kelvin. 25 °C is NOT 25 K. K = °C + 273. The V-vs-T trap (Jan 2026 Q13) exists purely to catch °C users.

×24 vs ×24 000

Volume in dm³ → ×24; in cm³ → ×24 000. 0.02 mol H₂ = 0.48 dm³ = 480 cm³. Mixing the two is a units error penalised once.

Total gas volume

After a reaction, count EVERY gaseous species: products AND leftover excess reactant. Jan 2026 Q12's trap: forgetting the leftover O₂ or the LHS volume loss.

Gas syringe vs over water

Insoluble gases (H₂) → over water OK. Soluble gases (CO₂, NH₃, SO₂) → gas syringe only. Solubility underestimates the volume.

Rounding to 2 SF

430 ÷ 24 000 = 0.0179 mol — rounding to 0.017 (2 SF) is penalised. Keep 3 SF unless the data says otherwise.

Measurement of the molar volume of a gas

$$\text{Mg} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2 + \text{H}_2 \qquad V_m = \frac{V(\text{H}_2)}{n(\text{Mg})}$$
🧪 Method
  • Weigh a known mass of Mg ribbon; add to excess HCl in a flask.
  • Collect the H₂ in an airtight gas syringe; record T and p.
  • Wait for the apparatus to cool to r.t.p., then read the volume.
  • $n(\text{H}_2) = n(\text{Mg}) = m/24.3$ (1 : 1); $V_m = V \div n$; compare with 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
🧮 Worked

0.0486 g Mg → n = 0.0486 ÷ 24.3 = 0.00200 mol; V = 48.0 cm³ = 0.0480 dm³ → V_m = 0.0480 ÷ 0.00200 = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. % error = (exp − true)/true × 100.

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Syringe must be airtight; excess acid ensures all Mg reacts (Mg = limiting).
  • Measure after cooling to r.t.p. — hot gas expands, V too big.
  • Ensure the gas is at atmospheric pressure (syringe open to air) — not "relieve pressure".
  • CO₂ dissolves in water → this gas must go in a syringe, never over water.
⚠️ Safety

HCl is an irritant — wear goggles; keep Mg away from naked flames (H₂ is flammable).

Spec 8.21 · CP3

Finding the concentration of a hydrochloric acid solution

$$\text{HCl} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad c(\text{HCl}) = \frac{n(\text{HCl})}{V(\text{HCl})}$$
🧪 Method
  • Pipette 25.0 cm³ HCl into a conical flask; add indicator (methyl orange).
  • Rough titration first, then accurate titrations — swirl constantly, add dropwise near the end.
  • Repeat until concordant (±0.1–0.2 cm³); discard the rough titre; mean the rest.
  • n(NaOH) = cV; 1 : 1 ratio → n(HCl); c(HCl) = n ÷ V.
🧮 Worked

Mean titre 24.00 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH → n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 24.00/1000 = 0.00240 mol = n(HCl) → c(HCl) = 0.00240 ÷ (25.0/1000) = 0.0960 mol dm⁻³.

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Wet burette → titre increased (acid diluted); wet pipette / flask → unchanged (same moles).
  • Endpoint for methyl orange: yellow → orange (with Na₂CO₃ + HCl).
  • Swirl = uniform mixture/concentration — "just mixing" is rejected.
  • % uncertainty: burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading → titre ±0.10 → 0.10/24.00 × 100 = 0.42%.
Spec 8.23 · CP4

Standard solution from a solid acid → find [NaOH]

$$\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad M_r = 126.1 \qquad c = \frac{n}{V}$$
🧪 Method
  • Weigh solid acid (e.g. oxalic acid dihydrate); dissolve in a beaker.
  • Transfer to a volumetric flask with washings; make up to the mark; invert to mix.
  • Pipette aliquots; titrate against NaOH; concordant mean titre.
  • n(acid) = mass/Mᵣ → c(acid); mole ratio → n(NaOH) → c(NaOH).
🧮 Worked

2.52 g H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O (Mᵣ 126.1) in 250 cm³ → n = 2.52/126.1 = 0.0200 mol → c = 0.0800 mol dm⁻³. 25.0 cm³ aliquot, titre 20.00 cm³ NaOH → n(acid) = 0.00200 → n(NaOH) = 0.00400 (1 : 2) → c(NaOH) = 0.200 mol dm⁻³.

💡 Examiner Tips
  • Extra water in the flask does not change the titre (moles unchanged) — reject "doesn't change concentration".
  • Dissolve first, then make up to the mark — never add the solid to a full flask.
  • Include water of crystallisation in Mᵣ (126.1, not 90.0).
  • Mean titre: discard the trial; (24.05 + 23.95 + 24.00)/3 = 24.00.
Spec 1.10 · gravimetric

Determining water of crystallisation (x in CuSO₄·xH₂O)

$$\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CuSO}_4 + x\text{H}_2\text{O} \qquad x = \frac{n(\text{H}_2\text{O})}{n(\text{anhydrous})}$$
🧪 Method
  • Weigh crucible + hydrated salt; heat strongly; cool in a desiccator; reweigh.
  • Repeat heat → cool → weigh until constant mass (all water driven off).
  • Mass water lost = initial − final; mol H₂O = m/18; mol anhydrous = m/Mᵣ; x = ratio.
🧮 Worked

2.50 g CuSO₄·xH₂O → 1.60 g anhydrous (Mᵣ 159.6): mol anhydrous = 1.60/159.6 = 0.01003; water = 0.90 g → 0.0500 mol; x = 0.0500/0.01003 = 4.99 ≈ 5 → CuSO₄·5H₂O.

💡 Examiner Tips
  • "Heat to constant mass" = repeat until no further mass change — "heat three times" is rejected.
  • Cool in a desiccator so the anhydrous salt doesn't reabsorb moisture.
  • Include the ·xH₂O in any Mᵣ asked for the hydrate itself.
Spec 1.8 · WCH13 Q4

Mᵣ of a gas / volatile liquid

$$n = \frac{V}{24\,000}\ (\text{cm}^3) \qquad M_r = \frac{m}{n}$$
🧪 Method
  • Vaporise a known mass of gas/volatile liquid into a gas syringe; measure the volume at r.t.p.
  • Ensure the syringe is at atmospheric pressure and cooled to r.t.p.
  • n = V ÷ 24 000 (cm³) or use pV = nRT for other conditions; Mᵣ = m/n.
🧮 Worked (WCH13 Jan 2026 Q4)

1.81 g gas occupies 750 cm³ at r.t.p.: n = 750 ÷ 24 000 = 0.03125 mol; Mᵣ = 1.81 ÷ 0.03125 = 58 → C₄H₁₀ (butane or 2-methylpropane).

💡 Examiner Tips
  • "Ensure at atmospheric pressure" — not "just to relieve pressure".
  • CO₂ dissolves in water → syringe, never over-water collection.
  • Error-chain (M1 → M2): loss in mass smaller → final mass higher → Mᵣ smaller.
  • Mᵣ 58 → C₄H₁₀; skeletal formula must match butane or 2-methylpropane.
⚠️ Safety

Hydrocarbons are flammable and harmful/irritant — no naked flames, work in a fume cupboard.

WCH13 is won on knowing which apparatus mistake changes the result — and by how much.

% uncertainty

% uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty ÷ reading) × 100. Worked: pipette ±0.04/25.0 × 100 = 0.16%; flask ±0.15/250 × 100 = 0.06%; burette ±0.05 per reading → 0.10/24.00 × 100 = 0.42%. Larger volumes ⇒ smaller % error.

Gas collection

Insoluble gas (H₂) → collect over water OK. Soluble gas (CO₂, NH₃, SO₂) → gas syringe only. Ensure the syringe is at atmospheric pressure and cooled to r.t.p. before reading.

Titration discipline

Rough titre first (approximate endpoint) → accurate titres dropwise with swirling → concordant within ±0.1–0.2 cm³ → discard the rough → mean the concordant values.

Gravimetric discipline

Heat → cool in desiccator → weigh → repeat to constant mass. Desiccator stops the anhydrous salt reabsorbing water vapour. "Heat three times" is not a reason — constant mass is.

⚠️ Confusion Pairs — Core Practicals & WCH13

Wet burette vs Wet pipette

Burette wet with water → titre increased (burette solution diluted). Pipette/flask wet → titre unchanged (same moles delivered). The classic 3-mark question.

Gas syringe vs Over water

Insoluble gas (H₂) → over water fine. Soluble gas (CO₂) → syringe only. Solubility underestimates the volume — the #1 CP1 gas trap.

% error vs % uncertainty

% error = (experimental − true)/true × 100 (accuracy vs accepted value). % uncertainty = (absolute ÷ reading) × 100 (instrument precision). Different questions, different formula.

"Heat three times" vs "constant mass"

The reason is constant mass (no further mass change = all water driven off), not the number of heatings. "Repeat three times" alone is rejected.

Rough vs Accurate titre

The rough titre is a fast approximation to find the endpoint zone — it is excluded from the mean. Only concordant accurate titres are averaged.

"Atmospheric pressure" vs "relieve pressure"

The syringe/container must be open to the atmosphere so the measured volume is at atmospheric pressure — "just to relieve pressure" scores nothing.
