Every assessment point elaborated with key learning goals and IAL examiner watch-outs. WCH12/01 · Unit 2 · 9A Kinetics + 9B Equilibria.

Spec 9.1 Collision factors

Effect of concentration, temperature, pressure & surface area on rate

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain rate changes using collision theory: more successful collisions per unit time → higher rate.
  • Concentration ↑ (solutions): more particles per unit volume → collision frequency ↑ → rate ↑.
  • Pressure ↑ (gases): particles closer → collision frequency ↑ → rate ↑ (not for pure solids/liquids under school conditions).
  • Surface area ↑ (solids, powder vs lump): more surface exposed → more surface collisions → rate ↑.
  • Temperature ↑: mainly a larger fraction of collisions have $E \ge E_a$ (see 9.4); frequency also rises slightly.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Weak answers only say “reacts faster”. You must link to collisions / frequency / energy. Pressure arguments apply to gases. Do not confuse rate with equilibrium yield (that is 9B). Raising $T$ does not lower $E_a$ — that is catalyst language.

Spec 9.2 Activation energy

Activation energy & successful collisions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define activation energy $E_a$ as the minimum energy required for a collision to result in reaction.
  • State that reactions only occur when collisions have sufficient energy ($E \ge E_a$).
  • Also require correct orientation of colliding particles (exam-complete answer).
  • Link higher rate to more successful collisions per unit time, not merely more collisions.
  • Know most collisions are unsuccessful under ordinary conditions.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Do not write “all collisions cause reaction”. Missing orientation loses a mark. Do not say temperature “gives molecules activation energy as a fixed gift” — $E_a$ is a threshold; distribution decides how many exceed it.

Spec 9.3 Measuring rate

Calculate rate from time and from graph gradients

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Use $\text{rate} = 1/t$ when timing to a fixed endpoint (same chemical change each run).
  • Obtain rate from a concentration/volume/mass–time graph as the gradient of a tangent.
  • Find initial rate from the tangent at $t = 0$.
  • Find rate at time $t$ from the tangent at that point on the curve.
  • State units consistent with axes (e.g. cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, mol dm$^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Use a tangent, not a chord between distant points. $1/t$ is invalid if different amounts of product form. Do not introduce rate equations, orders or $k$ (Topic 11). Large triangle on the tangent improves gradient accuracy.

Practise in: 📐 Tab 4 · Tangent Studio and ⏱️ Tab 5 · Rate Lab

Spec 9.4 Maxwell–Boltzmann & $T$

Temperature effect via Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Sketch the M–B curve: energy (x) vs fraction of molecules (y); peak = most probable energy; long high-energy tail.
  • Draw a vertical $E_a$ line; area to the right = molecules able to react.
  • At higher $T$: draw a second curve (peak lower and to the right); same total area; larger area past $E_a$.
  • Explain rate increase mainly by more collisions with $E \ge E_a$ (not only “more collisions”).
  • State total area under each curve represents all molecules (constant for fixed amount).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Classic mark scheme: two curves + one $E_a$ line. Do not draw $E_a$ horizontal. Do not change total area with $T$. Do not move $E_a$ when only temperature changes. Use Module 2 Live Curve to practise.

Spec 9.5 Role of catalysts

Catalysts provide a lower-$E_a$ alternative route

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State catalysts provide an alternative reaction route of lower activation energy.
  • Explain higher rate: more collisions are successful because less energy is needed.
  • Note the catalyst is not consumed overall (regenerated).
  • State $\Delta H$ / overall energy change of reaction is unchanged by a catalyst.
  • Recall catalysts increase rates of forward and reverse processes (equilibrium position unchanged — do not confuse with 9B yield).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Wrong: “catalyst gives molecules more energy” or “lowers $\Delta H$”. Correct phrase is almost always alternative route / lower $E_a$. Catalyst ≠ reactant in the net equation.

Spec 9.6 Reaction profiles

Draw uncatalysed vs catalysed profiles (with intermediate)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Sketch energy (y) vs reaction progress (x) for uncatalysed path: single peak; height from reactants = $E_a$.
  • On the same axes, catalysed path with lower $E_a$.
  • Show the energy level of the intermediate on the catalysed route (9.6 requirement).
  • Label reactants, products, $E_a$ (cat), $E_a$ (uncat), intermediate, and $\Delta H$.
  • Keep reactant and product energy levels the same for both paths so $\Delta H$ matches.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Forgetting the intermediate on the catalysed profile loses marks. Do not drop the product line when adding a catalyst. Practise Module 2 Profiles — compare the two vertical $E_a$ arrows.

Spec 9.7 Industry / sustainability

Catalysts make industrial processes more sustainable

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain that lower $E_a$ allows useful rates at lower temperature/pressure → less energy used.
  • Link catalysts to higher atom economy / fewer wasteful steps where relevant.
  • Give context examples (e.g. Haber Fe catalyst; Contact process V$_2$O$_5$) when asked.
  • Connect sustainability language: energy, waste, efficiency — not “faster” alone.
  • Note economic + environmental benefits of milder conditions.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Mark schemes want energy and/or atom economy, not only “reaction is faster”. Do not claim catalysts change the equilibrium constant / final yield position.

Spec 9.8 Catalyst on M–B

Interpret catalysts using Maxwell–Boltzmann curves

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • On a fixed-$T$ M–B curve, draw a lower $E_a$ for the catalysed route.
  • Explain larger area to the right of the lower $E_a$ → more successful collisions → higher rate.
  • State the catalyst does not change the shape of the energy distribution at that temperature.
  • Contrast with temperature: $T$ changes the curve; catalyst moves $E_a$.
  • Use correct phrasing: more molecules now have $E \ge E_a(\text{cat})$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Never say the catalyst “shifts the curve to the right” like temperature. Toggle “Show lower $E_a$ (catalyst)” on the Live Curve and compare the two vertical lines.

Spec 9.9 Dynamic equilibrium

Reversible reactions & dynamic equilibrium

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Know many reactions are readily reversible ($\rightleftharpoons$) and can reach equilibrium in a closed system.
  • State feature (i): the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction.
  • State feature (ii): the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant — constant, not necessarily equal.
  • Explain dynamic: both reactions continue at the molecular level; the reaction has not stopped.
  • Know the same equilibrium state is reached from either direction (starting with reactants or with products).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“The reaction stops at equilibrium” is wrong — rates are equal, not zero. Do not write [reactants] = [products]; the mark point is constant concentrations. Both features (i) and (ii) are usually needed for full marks.

Practise in: 📊 Tab 7 · Graph Studio — watch concentrations level off at Equilibrium I.

Spec 9.10 Le Chatelier

Predicting shifts in the position of equilibrium

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State the principle: when conditions change, the position of equilibrium shifts to counteract / oppose that change.
  • Concentration: add reactant → shift towards products; remove product → shift towards products.
  • Pressure (gases): increase → shift to the side with fewer moles of gas; equal moles → no effect on position.
  • Temperature: increase favours the endothermic direction; for an exothermic forward reaction the yield of products falls.
  • Catalyst: no change in position — forward and reverse rates increase equally, so equilibrium is reached faster.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Pressure answers must count gas moles on each side first — never “pressure always increases yield”. Say “position of equilibrium shifts”, not “equilibrium moves”. Quantitative $K$ treatment is Unit 4 — keep 9B answers qualitative. Practise in Engine E’s Live Predictor.

Spec 9.11 Industry · yield vs rate

Compromise conditions in industrial processes

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain the conflict for exothermic reversible reactions: low $T$ → high yield but slow rate; high $T$ → fast rate but low yield.
  • Evaluate data to justify a compromise temperature plus a catalyst (faster attainment of equilibrium, yield unchanged).
  • Justify pressure choices: high $P$ favours the fewer-moles side and raises rate, balanced against plant cost and safety.
  • Know the key cases: Haber (Fe, 450 °C, 200 atm) · Contact (V$_2$O$_5$, 450 °C, 1–2 atm) · ethene hydration (H$_3$PO$_4$, 300 °C, 60–70 atm).
  • Note recycling of unreacted feedstock raises overall yield without shifting the equilibrium.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Evaluate” needs both sides — rate and yield, plus cost/energy. Never write “450 °C gives the maximum yield” — it gives the best economic balance. A catalyst never raises the yield, only the speed of reaching it. Test your instincts in Engine F’s Compromise Builder.

Out of scope in this studio: Unit 4 Kinetics II (rate equations, orders, $k$, Arrhenius calculations) and Unit 4 Equilibria II ($K_c$ calculations, partial pressures, quantitative treatment). Keep 9B answers qualitative — “position of equilibrium” and yield language, not $K$ values.

Engine A · Syllabus 9.1–9.2

Collision Theory Learning Suite

5 concept cards
1. Collision theory & activation energy (9.1–9.2)
Successful collision $\Rightarrow$ $E \ge E_\text{a}$ and correct orientation

Collision theory: particles must collide to react. Most collisions are unsuccessful.

Activation energy $E_\text{a}$: minimum collision energy for bonds to break/rearrange so products can form.

Rate idea: more successful collisions per unit time $\Rightarrow$ higher rate.

Learning goals
  • State two conditions for a successful collision: sufficient energy ($\ge E_\text{a}$) and correct orientation.
  • Define activation energy in exam language.
  • Link reaction rate to frequency of successful collisions.
Examiner tips
  • Do not say “all collisions cause reaction”.
  • Orientation is a mark point — energy alone is incomplete.
  • Higher rate means more successful collisions per second, not necessarily more violent molecules only.
2. Concentration effect (solutions) (9.1)

Increase concentration of a solution reactant: more particles per unit volume.

Effect: collision frequency rises $\Rightarrow$ more successful collisions per unit time $\Rightarrow$ rate increases.

$E_\text{a}$: unchanged by concentration alone.

Learning goals
  • Explain concentration using collision frequency (particles per unit volume).
  • State that $E_\text{a}$ is not changed by concentration.
Examiner tips
  • Must link to collisions — “reacts faster” alone is weak.
  • Do not confuse with equilibrium yield (Topic 9B).
3. Pressure effect (gases) (9.1)

Increase pressure (same $T$, smaller volume or more gas moles): gas particles closer $\Rightarrow$ higher collision frequency.

Effect: rate increases for reactions involving gases.

Liquids/solids: pressure has negligible effect on rate under school conditions.

Learning goals
  • Link higher pressure to more gas particles per unit volume and higher collision frequency.
  • Know pressure mainly affects gas-phase reactions.
Examiner tips
  • Saying “pressure increases energy” is wrong — it is mainly frequency.
  • Do not apply pressure arguments to aqueous reactions without gas.
4. Surface area (solids) (9.1)

Powder vs lump: powder has larger surface area.

Effect: more solid surface exposed $\Rightarrow$ more collisions at the surface per unit time $\Rightarrow$ higher rate.

Examples: marble chips vs powder + acid; catalytic surface area in heterogeneous catalysis.

Learning goals
  • Explain SA via more exposed particles / surface collisions.
  • Compare powder and large pieces of the same solid mass.
Examiner tips
  • Mass alone does not set rate — exposed area does.
  • Link to heterogeneous catalysts when asked.
5. Temperature effect (collision view) (9.1, 9.4)

Increase $T$: particles move faster on average $\Rightarrow$ slightly higher collision frequency.

Main effect: much larger fraction of collisions have $E \ge E_\text{a}$ (see Maxwell–Boltzmann).

Result: rate increases strongly with temperature for many reactions.

Learning goals
  • Give both frequency and (mainly) fraction above $E_\text{a}$.
  • Know the dominant temperature effect is the high-energy tail of the distribution.
Examiner tips
  • Weak answer: “molecules collide more often” only — must mention energy / $E_\text{a}$.
  • Do not say $E_\text{a}$ decreases when $T$ rises (that is catalyst language).

Engine B · Syllabus 9.3

Measuring Rate of Reaction

Want the full practical toolkit? → 📐 Tab 4 · Tangent Studio (draw & verify tangents) · ⏱️ Tab 5 · Rate Lab ($1/t$ clock reactions)

1. Rate $= 1/\text{time}$ (9.3i)
$\text{rate} \propto \dfrac{1}{t}$    often written $\text{rate} = \dfrac{1}{t}$ for fixed-change comparisons

Use when a reaction is timed until a fixed observable endpoint (e.g. cross disappears, fixed volume of gas).

Shorter time $\Rightarrow$ higher rate for the same chemical change.

Units: if $t$ in s, $1/t$ has units s$^{-1}$ (comparative rate, not always concentration units).

Learning goals
  • Use $\text{rate}=1/t$ only for fixed-endpoint timing experiments.
  • Compare rates correctly: larger $1/t$ means faster reaction.
Examiner tips
  • Not valid if different amounts of product formed — change must be equivalent.
  • State units of time clearly.
2. Rate from graph gradient — initial rate (9.3ii)

Plot quantity vs time (e.g. concentration, volume of gas, mass loss).

Initial rate: draw a tangent at $t=0$, then $\text{rate} = |\text{gradient}| = \left|\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\right|$.

Initial rate avoids product interference and changing concentrations later.

Learning goals
  • Describe drawing a tangent at $t=0$ and calculating gradient.
  • Explain why initial rate is often preferred.
Examiner tips
  • Chord (joining two distant points) is not the same as a tangent at a point.
  • Use a large triangle for a more accurate gradient.
3. Rate from graph gradient — at time $t$ (9.3ii)

Draw a tangent to the curve at the required time $t$.

$\text{rate at } t = \left|\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\right|$ of that tangent.

Rate usually falls as reactants are used up (curve flattens).

Learning goals
  • Locate time $t$ on the curve and draw the tangent carefully.
  • Interpret decreasing gradient as decreasing rate.
Examiner tips
  • Read axes with units (e.g. cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, mol dm$^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$).
  • Do not invent rate equations / orders (Topic 11).
4. Experimental measures of progress

Common methods: gas volume vs time (syringe); mass loss vs time (CO$_2$ escape); titration of remaining reactant; colorimetry; “disappearing cross” turbidity.

Choose a method that tracks a clear, continuous change with time.

Learning goals
  • Name at least two practical methods for following rate.
  • Link the measured quantity to reactant/product change.
Examiner tips
  • Method must actually change with reaction progress.
  • Keep other variables controlled when comparing rates.

When to use which method?

  • 1/t: fixed visual/chemical endpoint, same change each run (clock reactions).
  • Tangent at t=0: continuous data; best single comparison of “how fast it starts”.
  • Tangent at t: how rate changes as reaction proceeds.

1. Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (9.4)

Shows the distribution of molecular energies at a given temperature.

Axes: energy (x) vs fraction / number of molecules (y). Curve starts at origin, rises to a peak (most probable energy), then long high-energy tail.

$E_\text{a}$ line: vertical line. Area to the right of $E_\text{a}$ = fraction of molecules that can react in a collision (energy enough).

Learning goals
  • Sketch the curve shape and mark $E_\text{a}$.
  • State that the area right of $E_\text{a}$ is the reactive fraction.
Examiner tips
  • Total area under curve represents all molecules (constant for fixed sample).
  • Do not draw $E_\text{a}$ as a horizontal line.
2. Effect of temperature on the curve (9.4)

At higher $T$: peak lower and shifts right; more molecules in the high-energy tail.

Area under each curve is the same (same number of molecules).

With fixed $E_\text{a}$, area right of $E_\text{a}$ increases a lot $\Rightarrow$ rate increases.

Learning goals
  • Draw two curves $T_1$ and $T_2$ ($T_2>T_1$) and one $E_\text{a}$ line.
  • Explain rate rise mainly via increased fraction above $E_\text{a}$.
Examiner tips
  • Exam classic: two curves + one $E_\text{a}$ line.
  • Do not move $E_\text{a}$ when only temperature changes.

Provides an alternative reaction route with lower activation energy.

Learning goals

Engine E · Syllabus 9.9–9.10

Dynamic Equilibrium & Le Chatelier Suite

4 concept cards + live predictor
1. Reversible reactions & dynamic equilibrium (9.9)
Dynamic equilibrium: rate(forward) $=$ rate(backward)  ·  concentrations constant

Reversible: many reactions can go both ways ($\rightleftharpoons$). In a closed system (nothing enters or leaves) they settle at equilibrium.

Dynamic: the reaction has not stopped — forward and backward reactions continue at equal rates, so there is no net change.

Same state from either direction: starting with pure reactants or pure products gives the same equilibrium mixture.

Learning goals
  • State both defining features: equal rates (i) and constant concentrations (ii).
  • Explain why “dynamic” — reactions continue at the molecular level.
  • State the closed-system requirement and the either-direction result.
Examiner tips
  • “The reaction stops” = lost mark. Rates are equal, not zero.
  • Constant concentrations ≠ equal concentrations — do not write [reactants] = [products].
2. Le Chatelier: concentration & catalyst (9.10)
Position shifts to counteract the change

Principle: when a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, the position of equilibrium shifts so as to oppose (counteract) that change.

Add reactant: position shifts towards products to use it up. Remove product: shifts towards products to replace it (industry removes product continuously, e.g. liquefying NH$_3$).

Catalyst: lowers $E_\text{a}$ for forward and backward equally → equilibrium reached faster, position and yield unchanged.

Learning goals
  • State the principle with the word “counteract / oppose”.
  • Predict shifts for adding/removing reactants or products in a homogeneous system.
  • State the exact catalyst result: faster attainment, no shift.
Examiner tips
  • “Catalyst shifts equilibrium to the right” is the single most common wrong phrase — never write it.
  • Say “position of equilibrium shifts”, not “the equilibrium moves”.
3. Le Chatelier: temperature & pressure (9.10)

Temperature ↑: favours the endothermic direction (absorbs the added heat). Exothermic forward reaction → position shifts to reactants → yield of products falls.

Pressure ↑ (gases): position shifts to the side with fewer moles of gas. Count only (g) coefficients. Equal moles (e.g. H$_2$ + I$_2$ $\rightleftharpoons$ 2HI) → no effect on position.

Rate is separate: $T$↑ always raises rate; $P$↑ raises rate for gases (closer particles) — even when the position does not move.

Learning goals
  • Predict and justify $T$ and $P$ effects for any given equation.
  • Always count gas moles before answering a pressure question.
  • Separate the position answer from the rate answer.
Examiner tips
  • For an exothermic forward reaction, “temperature ↑ decreases yield” is the mark point.
  • “Pressure ↑ increases yield” with no mole count = incomplete.
4. Demonstration equilibria (suggested practicals)
2NO$_2$(g) $\rightleftharpoons$ N$_2$O$_4$(g)  ·  brown $\rightleftharpoons$ pale yellow · exothermic forward

NO$_2$ / N$_2$O$_4$: heat the tube → darker brown (shift left, endothermic direction); cool it → paler (shift right); squeeze (pressure ↑) → paler (2 → 1 mol gas).

ICl + Cl$_2$ $\rightleftharpoons$ ICl$_3$: brown iodine(I) chloride $\rightleftharpoons$ orange iodine(III) chloride. Passing more Cl$_2$ (pressure ↑) → orange ICl$_3$; removing Cl$_2$ → back to brown ICl.

Safety: NO$_2$ and Cl$_2$ are toxic gases — teacher demonstration only, in a fume cupboard.

Learning goals
  • Link each colour observation to a shift direction.
  • Use these systems to illustrate $T$ and $P$ effects in explanations.
Examiner tips
  • Answer order: observation (colour) → shift direction → Le Chatelier reason.
  • State which side has fewer gas moles before concluding a pressure shift.

Engine F · Syllabus 9.11

Industrial Compromise: Yield vs Rate

4 process cards + builder
1. The yield–rate dilemma (9.11)
Exothermic reversible: low $T$ → high yield, slow rate · high $T$ → fast rate, low yield

Temperature conflict: for an exothermic forward reaction, cooling pushes the position to products (better yield) but makes the rate commercially useless; heating does the opposite.

Resolution: a compromise temperature + a catalyst — the catalyst makes the moderate temperature fast enough without touching the yield.

Pressure: when products have fewer gas moles, high $P$ raises both yield and rate — but compression costs and plant safety set the ceiling.

Recycling: unreacted feedstock is returned to the reactor, so overall yield is high even when per-pass conversion is low.

Learning goals
  • Explain both arms of the trade-off (rate vs equilibrium yield).
  • Define “compromise conditions” and why a catalyst is essential to them.
Examiner tips
  • “Evaluate” questions need both sides plus cost/energy — one-sided answers cap the mark.
  • Always say the catalyst speeds attainment of equilibrium; it never raises the yield.
2. Haber process — ammonia
N$_2$(g) + 3H$_2$(g) $\rightleftharpoons$ 2NH$_3$(g)   $\Delta H = -92$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ · 4 → 2 mol gas

Conditions: 450 °C · 200 atm · iron (Fe) catalyst.

Why 450 °C: exothermic forward — lower $T$ would give a higher yield but an uneconomically slow rate. 450 °C with Fe gives a practical rate and acceptable per-pass yield (~15%).

Why 200 atm: high pressure favours NH$_3$ (fewer-moles side) and raises the rate, but very high pressure needs expensive thick-walled plant and is dangerous — 200 atm is the economic optimum.

Yield boosters: NH$_3$ is liquefied and removed (position keeps shifting right); unreacted N$_2$/H$_2$ are recycled.

Learning goals
  • Write the equation with state symbols and $\Delta H$ sign.
  • Justify each of the three conditions in rate/yield/cost language.
Examiner tips
  • Never write “450 °C gives the maximum yield” — it gives the best economic balance.
  • Mention recycling when asked how overall yield is improved.
3. Contact process — sulfur trioxide
2SO$_2$(g) + O$_2$(g) $\rightleftharpoons$ 2SO$_3$(g)   $\Delta H = -196$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ · 3 → 2 mol gas

Conditions: 450 °C · 1–2 atm (slightly above atmospheric) · vanadium(V) oxide (V$_2$O$_5$) catalyst.

Why 450 °C: same compromise logic as Haber — exothermic forward, and below ~400 °C the uncatalysed rate is far too slow.

Why only ~1–2 atm: at 450 °C the equilibrium already lies far to the right (conversion ≈ 99%+), so compressing the gases would cost money for almost no extra yield.

Learning goals
  • Write the equation with state symbols and conditions.
  • Explain the low-pressure choice — the classic contrast with Haber.
Examiner tips
  • Compare-and-contrast questions love Haber (200 atm) vs Contact (~1 atm): the difference is how far right the equilibrium already lies.
  • Catalyst name: vanadium(V) oxide — V$_2$O$_5$.
4. Hydration of ethene — ethanol
C$_2$H$_4$(g) + H$_2$O(g) $\rightleftharpoons$ C$_2$H$_5$OH(g)   $\Delta H = -42$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ · 2 → 1 mol gas

Conditions: 300 °C · 60–70 atm (≈ 7 MPa) · phosphoric(V) acid (H$_3$PO$_4$) catalyst on silica.

Why 300 °C: exothermic forward — a compromise between yield (favoured by cold) and rate (favoured by heat).

Why 60–70 atm: fewer gas moles on the ethanol side, so pressure raises yield and rate — kept below higher values by cost and safety of high-pressure equipment.

Recycling: per-pass conversion is only ~5%, so unreacted ethene is recycled repeatedly.

Learning goals
  • Write the equation with state symbols, $\Delta H = -42$ kJ mol$^{-1}$, and conditions.
  • Explain why recycling is essential when per-pass conversion is low.
Examiner tips
  • Catalyst is H$_3$PO$_4$ (phosphoric acid) — not concentrated H$_2$SO$_4$ here.
  • Golden data: $\Delta H = -42$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ — use it to justify the temperature compromise.

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Kinetics & Equilibria

Maxwell-Boltzmann diagram

Must be ASYMPTOTIC (no long horizontal tail); reference the diagram in the explanation; shaded area = molecules exceeding Eₐ.

Catalyst ≠ position

A catalyst does NOT shift equilibrium position — only reaches it faster.

Shift → consequence

Always link the shift to the consequence: “shifts left → lower yield”. Stating the shift alone loses marks.

Compromise conditions

Explain industrial compromises (T, P, catalyst) on BOTH sides — rate AND yield AND cost.

Rate from graph

Rate = gradient of tangent at a point; not the mean rate.

Most probable vs number

Maxwell-Boltzmann: “most probable energy” ≠ “number of particles above Eₐ”.

📈 Measuring rate (9.3) — methods & exam steps

Clock / fixed change: $\text{rate} = 1/t$ (same endpoint each run). Shorter $t$ → larger rate.

Continuous graph: plot quantity vs time → rate = $|$gradient of tangent$|$.

  • Initial rate: tangent at $t=0$ (often used to compare conditions).
  • Rate at time $t$: tangent at that point; gradient usually falls as reactants are used up.
  • Units: from axes — e.g. cm$^3$ s$^{-1}$, g s$^{-1}$, mol dm$^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$.

Exam tip: Draw a large triangle on the tangent; show $\Delta y$ and $\Delta x$ in the working. Never use a chord and call it “rate at $t$”.

🧪 Catalysts & profiles (9.5–9.7)

Phrase to learn: “alternative reaction route of lower activation energy”.

Profile must show: uncatalysed peak · catalysed path · intermediate energy level · equal R and P levels · $\Delta H$ same · $E_a$ arrows clearly different heights.

Industry (9.7): lower $T/P$ possible → less energy; possible higher atom economy / less waste → more sustainable.

Exam tip: Catalyst speeds forward and reverse; equilibrium position / $K$ unchanged (do not write “shifts equilibrium to products”).

⚖️ Dynamic equilibrium (9.9) — the two mark points
Dynamic equilibrium: rate of forward reaction = rate of backward reaction · concentrations of reactants and products remain constant
  • Dynamic: both reactions continue at the molecular level — the reaction has not stopped (rates equal, not zero).
  • Closed system required — no reactant or product may escape.
  • The same equilibrium state is reached from either direction (pure reactants or pure products).
  • Constant concentrations ≠ equal concentrations.

Exam tip: 2-mark definitions usually want both features — equal rates (i) and constant concentrations (ii).

📝 IAL examination strategy (how marks are won)
  • Command words: “explain” needs collision/M–B language; “state” can be a short definition; “calculate” needs formula + units.
  • Explain rate change: factor → effect on collisions / energy → successful collisions per unit time → rate.
  • Diagrams: label axes; mark $E_a$ as a vertical line on M–B; on profiles mark $E_a$ as a vertical energy gap from reactants to TS.
  • Comparisons: higher $T$ vs catalyst — different mechanisms ($T$ reshapes curve; catalyst moves $E_a$).
  • Do not import Topic 11: no order, rate = $k[A]^n$, or Arrhenius number work unless the paper is later content.
  • Do not import 9B: yield, $K_c$, Le Chatelier are not rate explanations in 9A.
  • Practical: control variables when comparing rates; fixed endpoint for $1/t$.
⚠️ Master trap checklist (last-minute)
  • Successful collisions: energy and orientation.
  • Temperature: fraction above $E_a$ (M–B), not only “more collisions”.
  • Do not move $E_a$ when only $T$ changes; catalysts lower $E_a$.
  • Tangents not chords; $1/t$ only for equivalent endpoints.
  • Catalyst does not change $\Delta H$ or equilibrium position.
  • Catalysed profile needs intermediate (9.6).
  • No rate equations / orders in pure 9A answers.
  • Pressure: gases; surface area: solids; concentration: solutions.
  • M–B: $E_a$ is vertical; total area conserved when $T$ changes.
  • Wrong phrase: “catalyst gives particles energy”.
  • Equilibrium: rates equal and not zero — reaction continues (dynamic).
  • Constant concentrations ≠ equal concentrations (9.9).
  • Catalyst at equilibrium: no shift, no yield change — faster attainment only.
  • Pressure shifts: count gas moles first; equal moles → no shift (H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI).
  • $T$ ↑ with exothermic forward: yield ↓ even though rate ↑ — keep position and rate separate.
  • 450 °C (Haber) is a compromise — never “maximum yield”; add recycling for full marks.
  • Contact process runs at ~1–2 atm because conversion is already ≈99%.
