Spec 4.1 · 4.2 · 4.3 4A · Safety

Hazard vs risk, and making organic practicals safer

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define hazard (something with the potential to cause harm) vs risk (the likelihood that harm actually occurs, given exposure and conditions).
  • Recall typical organic hazards: flammable (low flash points), toxic, volatile / irritant vapours.
  • Explain why a risk assessment precedes any work with hazardous organics: identify the hazard, who could be harmed, exposure routes, and control measures.
  • Reduce risk by: (i) working on a smaller scale, (ii) precautions specific to the hazard (fume cupboard, no naked flames, heat via water bath, gloves + goggles), (iii) an alternative, less hazardous method.
  • Apply to this topic: alkanes are fuels → keep away from ignition sources; halogenation uses toxic $\text{Cl}_2/\text{Br}_2$ → fume cupboard.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Swapping the definitions of hazard and risk is a classic lost mark. "Take care" or "be careful" scores zero — always name a specific control and tie it to the named hazard (flammable → no naked flames / water bath; toxic / volatile → fume cupboard).

Spec 4.4 4A · Core ideas

Homologous series & functional groups

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define a homologous series: a family of compounds with the same functional group, the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and successive members differing by a –CH₂– group.
  • Physical properties change gradually with chain length (e.g. boiling point rises as London forces strengthen).
  • Define functional group: the atom or group of atoms responsible for the characteristic reactions of the series.
  • Apply: methane → ethane → propane each add one –CH₂–; all alkanes combust and undergo free radical substitution because they share the same C–C / C–H single-bond framework.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Jan 2025 Q21 mark scheme: members "have the same functional group(s)", "similar chemical properties", "the same general formula" and "differ from each other by a –CH₂– group" — a 2-mark question usually wants any two of these. Writing "similar physical properties" is wrong — physical properties change gradually.

Spec 4.5 4A · Nomenclature

IUPAC naming & molecular / displayed / skeletal formulae

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Know the alkyl prefixes to C10: meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-, hept-, oct-, non-, dec-.
  • Name branched alkanes: find the longest chain (parent), number from the end nearer the first branch, give substituents locants, list them alphabetically, use di-/tri- with commas between numbers.
  • Distinguish the three formula types: molecular ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$), displayed (every bond drawn), skeletal (zig-zag lines; vertices and line ends = carbons, H atoms implied).
  • Convert freely between skeletal ↔ displayed ↔ molecular for any spec alkane.
  • Name cycloalkanes: cyclo- prefix + parent alkane; a single substituent needs no number (methylcyclopropane).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"3-methylbutane" does not exist — renumbering gives 2-methylbutane. Count the longest chain through branches that look like they stick out sideways. Format: commas between numbers (2,3-), hyphens between numbers and letters (2-methyl). In displayed formulae, carbon must have exactly four bonds — a fifth bond voids the structure. Drill all of this in the Naming tab.

Spec 4.6 4A · Reaction types

Classify reactions: addition · substitution · oxidation · reduction · polymerisation

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Addition: two molecules join to form a single product (alkene + $\text{Br}_2$ — the Topic 5 showcase).
  • Substitution: an atom or group is replaced by another — alkane + $\text{Cl}_2$ (UV) is the Topic 4 example.
  • Oxidation: gain of O / loss of H / increase in oxidation number — combustion of alkanes is oxidation.
  • Reduction: loss of O / gain of H / decrease in oxidation number.
  • Polymerisation: many small monomers join into one very large molecule (addition polymerisation, Topic 5).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Students miss that combustion = oxidation. Alkane halogenation is substitution, never addition (there is no C=C to add across). Cracking is thermal decomposition — it is not one of the five named types, so don't force it into them.

Spec 4.7 – 4.8 4A · Bond fission

Homolytic vs heterolytic fission; free radicals & electrophiles

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Homolytic fission: the covalent bond breaks symmetrically — one electron to each atom → two free radicals. Favoured by UV light, gas phase, non-polar bonds (e.g. $\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} 2\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$).
  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one atom → a cation + an anion. Favoured by polar bonds and polar solvents.
  • Define free radical: a species with a single / unpaired electron, shown as one dot (Jan 2026 mark scheme wording).
  • Define electrophile: an electron pair acceptor — an electron-deficient species (e.g. $\text{H}^{+}$); the star of Topic 5 mechanisms.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Drawing a full curly arrow (electron pair) where a half (fishhook) arrow (single electron) is required costs the mechanism mark. $\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$ is a radical, not an electrophile. "$\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{Cl}^{+} + \text{Cl}^{-}$ under UV" is heterolysis — instant zero in a radical mechanism question.

Spec 4.9 4B · Structure

Alkanes & cycloalkanes — saturated hydrocarbons

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Hydrocarbon = a compound containing carbon and hydrogen only.
  • Saturated = contains single bonds only (all σ bonds) — no C=C.
  • General formulae: acyclic alkanes $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$; cycloalkanes $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$.
  • Test a formula: $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$ fits $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$ → alkane; $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$ fits $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ → cycloalkane or alkene (Topic 5).
  • Each carbon is tetrahedral (109.5°); the molecules are non-polar → only London (dispersion) forces between them.
  • Alkanes are relatively unreactive: strong C–C and C–H σ bonds, and non-polar bonds are not attacked by ions or polar reagents.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Cycloalkanes are saturated despite $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ — the ring removes two H atoms; there is no π bond. Do not write alkanes as "reactive" — exam answers must say they are unreactive because of the strong C–C / C–H bonds and their non-polarity (two mark points).

Spec 4.10 – 4.11 4B · Isomerism

Structural isomerism — draw & name isomers up to C6

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define structural isomers: compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae (atoms connected in a different order).
  • Memorise the alkane counts: $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$ → 2, $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$ → 3, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}$ → 5.
  • Systematic method: draw the straight chain → shorten it by one C and move a methyl branch along → shorten again and place two methyls → delete duplicates created by symmetry.
  • Draw and name cycloalkanes: cyclopropane → cyclohexane, plus substituted rings (methylcyclopropane, $\text{C}_4\text{H}_8$).
  • Know that cycloalkanes are structural isomers of alkenes (both $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$) — e.g. five cyclic isomers of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The most common error is drawing the same molecule rotated or flipped and counting it twice — 2-methylbutane mirrored is still 2-methylbutane. In $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}$, students lose 2,2-dimethylbutane and 2,3-dimethylbutane. Definition answers must say "same molecular formula, different structural formulae" — swapping the words scores zero.

Spec 4.12 4B · Crude oil

Alkanes as fuels — fractional distillation, cracking & reforming

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons (mainly alkanes); alkanes are used as fuels.
  • Fractional distillation separates fractions by boiling point — a physical process (no equation): refinery gas at the top → bitumen at the bottom; short chains = low boiling points, weak London forces.
  • Cracking = thermal decomposition of long-chain alkanes → shorter alkane + alkene: e.g. $\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_{22} \rightarrow \text{C}_8\text{H}_{18} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_4$; catalytic ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3/\text{SiO}_2$, ~450 °C) or thermal/steam (~800 °C).
  • Why crack: demand for short-chain petrol fraction exceeds supply; alkenes are feedstocks for polymers.
  • Reforming = straight-chain alkanes → branched and cyclic hydrocarbons $+ \text{H}_2$ (Pt/$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) — branched chains burn more smoothly (better octane rating).
  • Suggested practical: vaporise liquid paraffin over heated aluminium oxide; the gaseous products decolourise bromine water (alkene formed — Topic 5 test).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Calling fractional distillation a chemical reaction loses the mark — it is physical separation. Cracking equations must balance atoms and should include an alkene ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$); "alkanes only" as products is incomplete. Don't confuse cracking (break C–C, heat) with reforming (rearrange the skeleton, catalyst).

Spec 4.13 – 4.14 4B · Pollution

Combustion pollutants and the problems they cause

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • List the pollutants from alkane-fuel combustion: CO, oxides of nitrogen, sulfur dioxide, carbon particulates (soot) and unburned hydrocarbons.
  • CO forms by incomplete combustion; it is toxic because it binds to haemoglobin, blocking oxygen transport in the blood.
  • NOₓ: $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ from the air combine at engine temperature / spark; acidic oxides → acid rain; also photochemical smog.
  • SO₂ comes from sulfur impurities in the fuel; acidic oxide → acid rain ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$ / $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
  • Particulates → respiratory damage; unburned hydrocarbons + NOₓ + sunlight → smog.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

CO₂ is not on the pollutant list here — it is the greenhouse gas of 4.15. For CO, the mark point is haemoglobin binding, not just "poisonous". The nitrogen in NOₓ comes from the air, not the fuel; the sulfur in SO₂ comes from fuel impurities, not the air — examiners swap these to catch you.

Spec 4.15 – 4.16 4B · Future fuels

Alternative fuels, sustainability & carbon neutrality

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Why develop alternatives: crude oil is finite; combustion CO₂ enhances the greenhouse effect → climate change; pollutants damage health.
  • Define carbon neutral: no net release of CO₂ to the atmosphere over the full fuel cycle.
  • Bioethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$, from fermentation): CO₂ released on combustion ≈ CO₂ absorbed while the plants grew → approximately carbon neutral (farming and distillation burn fossil energy).
  • Hydrogen: $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ — zero CO₂ at point of use; but manufacture (steam reforming of methane emits CO₂; electrolysis depends on the electricity source) and storage/flammability are drawbacks.
  • Petrol: adds CO₂ that was locked underground → not carbon neutral.
  • Evaluate any fuel claim over its whole cycle, not just the combustion step.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"Hydrogen is carbon neutral" is only true if manufactured renewably — always qualify it. Bioethanol is "approximately" neutral — the qualification is the mark. Do not confuse zero CO₂ at combustion with carbon neutral (manufacture counts). For full marks on hydrogen, also give a disadvantage (storage, flammability, production CO₂).

Spec 4.17 4B · Reactions i

Combustion of alkanes — complete & incomplete

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Complete combustion (excess $\text{O}_2$): alkane $+ \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$; highly exothermic — the reason alkanes are excellent fuels.
  • Incomplete combustion (limited $\text{O}_2$): $\rightarrow \text{CO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, or with very limited air $\rightarrow \text{C}$ (soot) $+ \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
  • Balance combustion equations for any alkane — drill it in the Equation Solver tab.
  • Flame character: clean blue flame = complete; yellow, sooty flame = incomplete.
  • The other key alkane reaction: halogens in UV light — see the Mechanism tab (4.18).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

State excess / limited oxygen when explaining which products form — it is a mark point. Unbalanced combustion equations lose the equation mark; water is a product and students keep forgetting it. Combustion is an oxidation, not a substitution.

Spec 4.18 4B · Reactions ii

Free radical substitution — the full mechanism

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Overall: alkane + halogen $\xrightarrow{\text{UV}}$ halogenoalkane + HX; e.g. $\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$.
  • Initiation: $\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} 2\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$ — homolytic fission; draw curly half-arrows, one electron to each Cl.
  • Propagation: $\text{Cl}^{\bullet} + \text{CH}_4 \rightarrow {^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + \text{HCl}$ then ${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}^{\bullet}$ — a radical in, a radical out; the chain sustains itself.
  • Termination: any two radicals combine ($\text{Cl}^{\bullet}+\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$, $\text{Cl}^{\bullet}+{^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3}$, ${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3}+{^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$) — no new radical, chain ends.
  • Limitation (4.18 iv): further substitution ($\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CHCl}_3 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4$) gives a mixture and low yield of the monosubstituted product — use excess alkane to minimise it.
  • Every radical carries a single dot (the unpaired electron).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Jan 2025 & Jan 2026 mark schemes: initiation requires "ultraviolet light / ultraviolet radiation" (heat alone is not the phrase they want); a radical has "a single / unpaired electron"; propagation = radical + molecule → new radical + product; termination = "two (free) radicals join together". Forgetting the dot on a radical, or writing initiation without UV, are the two most common lost marks.

Scope note

Deferred to Topic 5 / Unit 2 — deliberately not taught here

Alkenes (C=C, E–Z isomerism, electrophilic addition mechanisms, bromine water test, addition polymerisation) belong to Topic 5; reactions of halogenoalkanes and alcohols belong to Unit 2 (WCH12). In this studio, alkenes appear only where the syllabus requires them — as cracking products (4.12) — and electrophiles appear only as the 4.8 definition.

Free Radical Substitution Learning Suite · Spec 4.7 · 4.8 · 4.18

Alkane + Halogen (UV) — Initiation · Propagation · Termination

7 Inspector Cards · 10 Classifier Steps
1. Overall: Methane + Chlorine $\xrightarrow{\text{UV}}$ Chloromethane
$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV light}} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$

Conditions: chlorine (or bromine) gas, UV light / sunlight, room temperature. Mechanism: free radical substitution. Observations: the green-yellow chlorine colour fades; steamy acidic fumes of HCl appear in moist air (white fumes with ammonia). Darkness: no reaction — the mixture is stable without UV.

Learning goals
  • State reagents ($\text{Cl}_2/\text{Br}_2$) and UV for free radical substitution of alkanes.
  • Name the organic product (halogenoalkane) and inorganic co-product (HX).
  • Classify the reaction as substitution (4.6).
Examiner tips
  • Missing "UV light / sunlight" is the most common lost mark.
  • Never call this electrophilic substitution or addition.
  • HCl is a product — include it.

Safety: chlorine and bromine are toxic/corrosive — fume cupboard; UV sources can damage eyes.

2. Initiation — Homolytic Fission
$\text{Cl}{-}\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} \text{Cl}^{\bullet} + \text{Cl}^{\bullet}$

What happens: UV provides the energy for homolytic fission — the shared pair splits symmetrically, one electron to each atom, forming two chlorine free radicals. Note the curly half-arrows (fishhooks): each shows the movement of a single electron.

Learning goals
  • Define homolytic fission and free radical (species with an unpaired electron).
  • Draw initiation with half-arrows, one electron per atom.
  • State that initiation is the only stage that requires UV.
Examiner tips
  • Full double-headed arrows here = heterolysis = wrong mechanism.
  • $\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{Cl}^{+} + \text{Cl}^{-}$ is never the initiation step.
  • Write a dot on every radical, every time.
3. Propagation ① — Hydrogen Abstraction
$\text{Cl}^{\bullet} + \text{CH}_4 \rightarrow {^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + \text{HCl}$

The chlorine radical abstracts a hydrogen atom (H with its one electron) from methane, forming hydrogen chloride and a methyl radical. A radical is consumed and a new radical is produced — that is the signature of propagation.

Learning goals
  • Write propagation ① for methane and for longer alkanes (e.g. ethane, Jan 2026 style).
  • Recognise the pattern: radical + molecule → new radical + stable molecule.
Examiner tips
  • The dot must move to the carbon: ${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3}$, not $\text{CH}_3^{\bullet}\text{H}$.
  • HCl forms here — not in propagation ②.
4. Propagation ② — Product Formation & Chain Carrier
${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}^{\bullet}$

The methyl radical attacks a chlorine molecule, forming chloromethane and regenerating a chlorine radical — which loops back to propagation ①. One photon of UV can therefore produce thousands of product molecules: a chain reaction.

Learning goals
  • Write propagation ② and identify the regenerated chain carrier ($\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$).
  • Explain why the reaction is self-sustaining once initiated.
Examiner tips
  • Students wrongly classify this as termination because a product forms — but a radical is also produced, so the chain continues.
  • Keep the dots balanced: one radical in, one radical out.
5. Termination — Three Ways the Chain Dies

$\text{Cl}^{\bullet} + \text{Cl}^{\bullet} \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2$

${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + \text{Cl}^{\bullet} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$

${^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} + {^{\bullet}\text{CH}_3} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_3$ (ethane)

Any collision between two radicals forms a stable molecule and removes reactive species without generating a replacement — the chain ends. The appearance of ethane in the product mixture is classic evidence for the methyl radical intermediate.

Learning goals
  • Write all three termination steps for methane chlorination.
  • State the defining feature: two radicals join; no new radical is formed.
  • Use ethane formation as mechanism evidence.
Examiner tips
  • A step with a radical on the product side is never termination.
  • Jan 2025 wording: "two (free) radicals join together / react".
6. Limitation — Further Substitution (Spec 4.18 iv)
$\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2,\,\text{UV}} \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2,\,\text{UV}} \text{CHCl}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2,\,\text{UV}} \text{CCl}_4 \;(+\,\text{HCl each step})$

Chloromethane still has three replaceable hydrogens, so chlorine radicals keep attacking: the products are a mixture of $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$, $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{CHCl}_3$ and $\text{CCl}_4$. This is why free radical substitution has limited use in synthesis — poor yield of any single product. Fix: use the alkane in large excess so $\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$ overwhelmingly meets $\text{CH}_4$, not $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$.

Learning goals
  • Name all four chloromethanes and state HCl forms at each stage.
  • Explain the mixture problem and the excess-alkane remedy.
Examiner tips
  • Jan 2026: the limitation mark is "there can be further substitution reactions / more than one substitution product".
  • "Excess alkane" is the standard remedy mark — learn the phrase.

Safety: chlorinated organics ($\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{CHCl}_3$, $\text{CCl}_4$) are toxic — halogenations run in a fume cupboard.

7. Past-Paper Style — Ethane (Jan 2026 Q24)
$\text{Cl}^{\bullet} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_3 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2^{\bullet} + \text{HCl}$

The same propagation pattern with ethane: abstraction gives an ethyl radical, which then reacts with $\text{Cl}_2$ to give chloroethane and regenerate $\text{Cl}^{\bullet}$. Termination coupling of two ethyl radicals gives butane — $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$.

Learning goals
  • Transfer the mechanism to any alkane, not just methane.
  • Predict the coupling product of two alkyl radicals (doubled carbon count).
Examiner tips
  • Place the dot on the end carbon: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2^{\bullet}$.
  • With propane, abstraction at different carbons gives two alkyl radicals → isomeric products (mixture again).

⚡ High-Yield Examiner Trap Pairs — Mechanism
Trap 1: Homolytic vs heterolytic fission

Homolytic: symmetric split, one electron each → radicals (UV, gas phase, non-polar $\text{Cl}{-}\text{Cl}$).
Heterolytic: both electrons to one atom → ions (polar bonds, polar solvents). Writing ions in a radical mechanism is an instant mechanism failure.

Trap 2: Half-arrows vs full arrows

Half (fishhook) arrow: movement of one electron — radical steps.
Full curly arrow: movement of an electron pair — ionic mechanisms (Topic 5 onwards). Initiation must be drawn with two fishhooks, one to each Cl.

Trap 3: UV light vs heat vs darkness

The mark scheme phrase is "ultraviolet light / UV radiation / sunlight". "Heat" is not accepted for initiation, and in the dark the alkane–halogen mixture does not react at all — a favourite 1-mark question.

Trap 4: Substitution vs addition

Alkanes (saturated) can only undergo substitution — an H is replaced by a halogen, HX co-product. Addition needs a C=C (alkenes, Topic 5): two molecules join, no co-product. If your equation has HCl on the right, it is substitution.

Trap 5: Excess alkane vs excess halogen

Excess alkane → monosubstituted product dominates (good synthesis). Excess halogen → progressive substitution to the fully halogenated product ($\text{CCl}_4$ from methane) — a mixture. Exam questions phrase this as "how would you maximise the yield of chloromethane?"

Fuels, Crude Oil & Pollution Learning Suite · Spec 4.12 – 4.17

From Crude Oil to CO₂ — Distillation · Cracking · Reforming · Combustion · Pollutants

9 Inspector Cards · 2 Decision Maps
1. Fractional Distillation — Physical Separation

Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons, mainly alkanes. It is vaporised and fed into a fractionating column: hot at the bottom, cool at the top. Fractions condense at different heights according to boiling point. Small molecules (refinery gas, petrol) leave near the top; large ones (diesel, fuel oil, bitumen) near the bottom.

Why the trend: longer chain → more electrons → stronger London forces → higher boiling point. Viscosity and flammability change the same way (short chains = runny + very flammable).

Learning goals
  • State that fractional distillation separates by boiling point — a physical change, no equation.
  • Order the main fractions and link chain length to boiling point via London forces.
  • Explain the supply problem: the petrol fraction is in higher demand than the amount crude oil naturally provides — the reason for cracking.
Examiner tips
  • Never call it a chemical reaction — bonds are not broken.
  • "Molecules rise because they are light" is vague; say they condense where the temperature matches their boiling point.
2. Cracking — Breaking Long Chains
$\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_{22} \xrightarrow{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3/\text{SiO}_2,\;\sim450^{\circ}\text{C}} \text{C}_8\text{H}_{18} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_4$

Definition: thermal decomposition of long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful molecules. Products: a shorter alkane + an alkene (or alkene + $\text{H}_2$). Catalytic cracking: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3/\text{SiO}_2$, ~450 °C. Thermal/steam cracking: ~800 °C, no catalyst. Why: matches supply to demand for the petrol fraction; alkenes are polymer feedstocks.

Suggested practical: vaporise liquid paraffin over heated aluminium oxide catalyst; pass the gaseous products through bromine water — it decolourises, proving an alkene formed (Topic 5 test).

Learning goals
  • Write and balance a cracking equation (any valid alkane + alkene split).
  • State both conditions routes and both industrial reasons.
  • Describe the school practical and its bromine-water observation.
Examiner tips
  • Balance atoms — C and H must both check out.
  • Products must include an alkene ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$); "two shorter alkanes" does not balance hydrogen.
  • Build any equation instantly in the Equation Solver tab.

Safety: cracking practical — hot catalyst tube and flammable alkane/alkene vapours: heat the catalyst first, keep flames away from the collection end, and vent or collect over water in a fume cupboard.

3. Reforming — Reshaping the Skeleton
$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14} \xrightarrow{\text{Pt}/\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3,\;\Delta} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\,(\text{cyclohexane}) + \text{H}_2$

Reforming converts straight-chain alkanes into branched-chain and cyclic hydrocarbons (hydrogen is a co-product). Branched and cyclic molecules burn more smoothly in engines — fewer "knocks" — so reforming raises fuel quality (octane rating). Catalyst: platinum on aluminium oxide, heated.

Learning goals
  • State the purpose of reforming: straight → branched/cyclic, better combustion quality.
  • Contrast with cracking: reforming rearranges the carbon skeleton; cracking breaks it.
Examiner tips
  • Swapping "cracking" and "reforming" in a definition question is a classic examiner swap.
  • $\text{H}_2$ co-product is a useful mark point for the cyclic example.
4. Complete Combustion — Why Alkanes Are Fuels
$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$   (excess $\text{O}_2$)

With a plentiful oxygen supply, every carbon becomes $\text{CO}_2$ and every hydrogen becomes $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. The reaction is strongly exothermic — large energy release per mole — which is exactly why alkanes (methane in natural gas, propane in LPG, octane in petrol) are prized as fuels. Clean blue flame.

Learning goals
  • Write and balance complete combustion for any alkane (Solver tab drills this).
  • Link the exothermic enthalpy change to the usefulness of alkanes as fuels.
  • Classify combustion as oxidation (4.6).
Examiner tips
  • State "excess oxygen / plentiful air" as the condition.
  • Do not omit water — it is half the products.
5. Incomplete Combustion — CO and Soot

$2\text{CH}_4 + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$  (limited $\text{O}_2$)

$\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{C} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$  (very limited $\text{O}_2$)

With limited oxygen, carbon is only partially oxidised: carbon monoxide (toxic gas) or carbon (soot — yellow, smoky flame, black deposits). Water still forms. In car engines and faulty heaters, both regimes occur — which is why exhaust contains CO and particulates.

Learning goals
  • Write both incomplete equations and state "limited oxygen".
  • Link sooty flame appearance to incomplete combustion.
Examiner tips
  • The condition mark is "limited / insufficient oxygen" — never just "burning".
  • CO is odourless and colourless — "smells of burning" is wrong.
6. Oxides of Nitrogen — The Air Is the Source

$\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{spark / high }T\text{ in engine}} 2\text{NO}$

$2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$

At the extreme temperature and spark inside an engine, nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine — the fuel contains no nitrogen. NO is then oxidised to brown $\text{NO}_2$. Both are acidic oxides: they dissolve in rainwater forming nitric acid → acid rain; $\text{NO}_2$ also drives photochemical smog and irritates lungs.

Learning goals
  • Write the engine formation equations for NO and NO₂.
  • State the source (air, not fuel), the problem (acid rain, smog) and the acidic-oxide character.
Examiner tips
  • "Nitrogen comes from the fuel" is the trap — it comes from the air.
  • Give the reason for formation: the high temperature / spark provides the activation energy.
7. Sulfur Dioxide, Particulates & Unburned Hydrocarbons
$\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$   then   $\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$

SO₂: sulfur impurities in the fuel burn with the fuel — an acidic oxide → sulfurous/sulfuric acid in rain → acid rain (corrodes limestone buildings, acidifies lakes). Carbon particulates: unoxidised carbon from incomplete combustion — fine soot lodges in lungs. Unburned hydrocarbons: fuel that never ignited; with NOₓ and sunlight they form photochemical smog.

Learning goals
  • Match each pollutant to its source, problem and remedy (Module 2 map drills this).
  • State SO₂'s origin: sulfur impurities in the fuel — removable before combustion.
Examiner tips
  • Examiners swap the NOₓ and SO₂ sources to catch you: NOₓ ← air; SO₂ ← fuel impurity.
  • "Acidic oxide" is the phrase that earns the acid-rain link.
8. The Catalytic Converter — Cleaning Exhaust
$2\text{CO} + 2\text{NO} \xrightarrow{\text{Rh / Pt catalyst}} 2\text{CO}_2 + \text{N}_2$

A honeycomb coated with rhodium / platinum catalysts converts the two worst exhaust gases into harmless ones: CO is oxidised to $\text{CO}_2$ while NO is reduced to $\text{N}_2$ — in the same pass, unburned hydrocarbons are oxidised to $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.

Learning goals
  • Write the converter equation and name the catalyst metals.
  • State which pollutants it removes (CO, NOₓ, unburned HC) and which it does not (SO₂, CO₂).
Examiner tips
  • The converter does nothing about CO₂ (greenhouse) or SO₂ (remove sulfur from fuel first) — a favourite true/false trap.
9. Alternative Fuels & Carbon Neutrality (4.15–4.16)

$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (bioethanol)

$2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (hydrogen)

Carbon neutral = no net release of CO₂ over the fuel's whole cycle. Bioethanol: plants absorb CO₂ while growing ≈ CO₂ released on combustion → approximately neutral (farming and distillation use fossil energy, so not perfectly). Hydrogen: only water at point of use, but manufacture by methane reforming emits CO₂ and electrolysis is only as green as its electricity; storage (low density, embrittlement) and flammability are real drawbacks. Petrol: releases ancient carbon → not neutral.

Learning goals
  • Define carbon neutrality and apply it to petrol, bioethanol and hydrogen.
  • Give one advantage and one disadvantage per fuel — extended answers demand both.
Examiner tips
  • Always qualify: bioethanol is "approximately" neutral; hydrogen is neutral only if made renewably.
  • "Zero CO₂ at combustion" ≠ "carbon neutral" — manufacture counts.

⚡ High-Yield Examiner Trap Pairs — Fuels & Pollution
Trap 1: Distillation vs cracking vs reforming

Distillation: physical separation by boiling point — no bonds broken.
Cracking: breaks C–C bonds; long → short + alkene.
Reforming: rearranges the skeleton; straight → branched/cyclic + H₂. One word each: separate / break / reshape.

Trap 2: Complete vs incomplete combustion

The deciding factor is the oxygen supply, not the fuel. Same fuel, excess air → CO₂ + H₂O (blue flame); limited air → CO or C + H₂O (yellow, sooty flame). Always state the oxygen condition in answers.

Trap 3: CO vs CO₂

CO: toxic pollutant (haemoglobin), from incomplete combustion — removed by the catalytic converter.
CO₂: not toxic at these levels; a greenhouse gas driving climate change — not removed by the converter. Examiners love asking which one the converter fixes.

Trap 4: Carbon neutral vs zero-CO₂ combustion

Hydrogen burns with zero CO₂ but is only carbon neutral if manufactured renewably (steam reforming of methane emits CO₂). Bioethanol does release CO₂ when burned — it is neutral only because the plants absorbed it first. "Carbon neutral" is a whole-cycle claim.

1. Structural Isomerism — Definition + Butane Family

Structural isomers: compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae — the atoms are connected in a different order. For $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$:

butane (b.p. −0.5 °C)

2-methylpropane (b.p. −11.7 °C)

Learning goals
  • Recite the definition word-perfect: same molecular formula, different structural formulae.
  • Draw and name both C4 isomers.
  • Note branching lowers boiling point (smaller surface contact → weaker London forces).
Examiner tips
  • Swapping "molecular" and "structural" in the definition scores zero.
  • Isomers have different physical but similar chemical properties (same functional group).
2. Pentane Family — $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$ (3 isomers)

pentane

2-methylbutane

2,2-dimethylpropane

Learning goals
  • Draw all three from memory — the most-rewarded isomer set in Paper 1.
  • Apply the naming rules: longest chain, lowest locants, commas and hyphens.
Examiner tips
  • "3-methylbutane" and "1-methylpropane" are not isomers — they are misnamed pentane and butane.
  • Boiling point order: pentane > 2-methylbutane > 2,2-dimethylpropane (branching ↓ surface contact).
3. Hexane Family — $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{14}$ (5 isomers)

hexane

2-methylpentane

3-methylpentane

2,2-dimethylbutane

2,3-dimethylbutane

Learning goals
  • Use the systematic sweep: straight chain → methyl on a 5-chain (positions 2, 3) → two methyls on a 4-chain (2,2- and 2,3-).
  • Verify each structure has exactly 6 carbons and 14 hydrogens.
Examiner tips
  • The two lost isomers are almost always 2,2-dimethylbutane and 2,3-dimethylbutane.
  • "4-methylpentane" = 2-methylpentane flipped; "2-ethylbutane" = 3-methylpentane misnamed — check the longest chain again.
4. Cycloalkanes — $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ but Saturated

cyclopropane C3H6

cyclobutane C4H8

cyclopentane C5H10

cyclohexane C6H12

Learning goals
  • General formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ — closing the ring removes two H atoms, not a π bond.
  • Cycloalkanes are saturated: single bonds only, like open-chain alkanes.
  • Name them: cyclo- + parent; one substituent needs no number.
Examiner tips
  • "Cycloalkanes are unsaturated because CnH2n" is the trap — unsaturation means a C=C, which rings do not have.
5. Substituted Rings — Five Cyclic Isomers of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$

cyclopentane

methylcyclobutane

ethylcyclopropane

1,1-dimethylcyclopropane

1,2-dimethylcyclopropane

Learning goals
  • Systematic ring sweep: 5-ring → 4-ring + methyl → 3-ring + ethyl or two methyls (1,1- and 1,2-).
  • Name substituted rings with lowest locants (1,1- before 1,2-).
Examiner tips
  • This exact family appeared in a Paper 1 deduction question (unknown hydrocarbon C5H10) — listing all five cyclic isomers was the full-mark path.
6. Cycloalkanes ↔ Alkenes — Shared $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$

Because alkenes also follow $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$, every cycloalkane is a structural isomer of the corresponding alkenes: $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$ could be cyclopentane or pent-1-ene, pent-2-ene, 2-methylbut-1-ene, 3-methylbut-1-ene, 2-methylbut-2-ene. Topic 5 preview: the two families are told apart chemically — alkenes decolourise bromine water; cycloalkanes do not.

Learning goals
  • Given CnH2n, list both possibilities: cyclic saturated or open-chain unsaturated.
  • State how bromine water distinguishes them (observation belongs to Topic 5).
Examiner tips
  • When a question gives "molecular formula C5H10" and asks for isomers, check whether it wants all structural isomers (rings + alkenes) or only one family — read the stem twice.

⚡ High-Yield Examiner Trap Pairs — Isomerism
Trap 1: Isomer vs the same molecule redrawn

Rotating or flipping a structure does not make a new isomer. Test: rename the "new" structure by IUPAC rules — if the name is identical (e.g. "3-methylbutane" → 2-methylbutane), it is the same compound. Count names, not drawings.

Trap 2: Longest chain through the corner

In skeletal drawings the longest chain can bend a corner. "2-ethylbutane" is really a 5-carbon chain → 3-methylpentane. Always trace every possible path before naming.

Trap 3: Numbering direction

Number from the end nearer the first branch: 2-methylbutane, never 3-methylbutane. With two substituents, lowest set of locants wins (2,2- and 2,3- both fine; 3,3- would be wrong).

Trap 4: CnH2n — ring or double bond?

$\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$ could be a cycloalkane (saturated ring) or an alkene (C=C). They are structural isomers of each other. The formula alone cannot decide — you need a chemical test (bromine water, Topic 5) or more data.
