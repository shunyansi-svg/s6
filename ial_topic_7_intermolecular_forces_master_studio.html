Every WCH12 Topic 7 code elaborated — the forces between molecules, and how they dictate boiling points, volatility and solubility. See the forces move in the Dipole Lab (Tab 2).

Spec 7.1i London Forces

1. London (Instantaneous Dipole–Induced Dipole) Forces

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • London forces exist between all atoms and molecules — polar and non-polar alike.
  • Electrons are constantly moving: at any instant the cloud can be lopsided, creating an instantaneous dipole.
  • An instantaneous dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule by repelling/attracting its electrons.
  • The resulting weak attraction is the instantaneous dipole–induced dipole force (London force).
  • Strength increases with more electrons (larger relative molecular mass) and greater surface area for contact (long, unbranched chains).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Write the full name "instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces" — "van der Waals" is accepted but vague, and "London forces" alone is safest paired with the mechanism. The dipoles are temporary and shifting, never fixed. Do not say London forces get stronger because "bonds" get bigger — it is about electron count and contact area.

Spec 7.1ii Permanent Dipole–Dipole

2. Permanent Dipole–Permanent Dipole Interactions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Polar molecules (e.g. HCl, CH₃Cl) carry a permanent dipole because of electronegativity differences (Topic 3 link).
  • The $\delta^+$ end of one molecule attracts the $\delta^-$ end of a neighbour — a permanent dipole–permanent dipole interaction.
  • Molecules rotate to align opposite partial charges, though thermal motion constantly disturbs the alignment.
  • These are stronger than London forces for molecules of similar size, but far weaker than covalent bonds.
  • Polar molecules also have London forces — the total attraction is the sum of both.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A common script error: saying polar molecules have only dipole–dipole forces. They always have London forces too — and for large molecules the London part can dominate (e.g. C₈H₁₈ boils higher than CH₃OH). Keep $\delta^+ / \delta^-$ notation for partial charges; full charges (+/−) imply ions.

Spec 7.1iii Hydrogen Bonding

3. Hydrogen Bonding — the Strongest IMF

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • A hydrogen bond forms when H is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom — F, O or N only.
  • The H–F/O/N bond is highly polar: H carries a strong $\delta^+$ and is small enough to approach closely.
  • The $\delta^+$ H is attracted to a lone pair on an F, O or N atom of a neighbouring molecule.
  • Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular force (~20–40 kJ mol⁻¹) — but still only ~5–10% of a covalent bond.
  • The interaction is strongest when the three atoms line up (~180°): donor–H ⋯ lone pair.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A hydrogen bond is NOT a covalent bond — it is an intermolecular attraction, drawn as a dotted line. HCl, H₂S and CH₄ do not hydrogen-bond: Cl and S are not electronegative/small enough, and C–H is not polar enough. Both conditions are mark points: H on F/O/N and a lone pair on F/O/N.

Spec 7.2 H₂O · NH₃ · HF

4. Hydrogen Bonding in Water, Ammonia & HF

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Water: each molecule has 2 H atoms (δ+) and 2 lone pairs on O — it can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds, building an extensive network.
  • Ammonia: 3 H atoms but only 1 lone pair on N — fewer effective H-bonds per molecule than water.
  • Hydrogen fluoride: 1 H atom and 3 lone pairs on F — again limited to fewer H-bonds per molecule than water.
  • Water's ability to form more H-bonds per molecule explains its exceptionally high boiling point among the hydrides.
  • Draw H-bonds as dotted lines from H(δ+) of one molecule to the lone pair of F/O/N on another.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

In diagrams, the dotted H-bond line must start at the H atom and end at the lone pair of the other molecule — not at the O nucleus. Examiners reward the phrase "lone pair on the oxygen of a neighbouring molecule". NH₃ and HF H-bond, but water H-bonds most extensively (2 H + 2 lone pairs).

Spec 7.3 Anomalous Water

5. Water's Anomalous Properties

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • High melting/boiling point: H₂O (100 °C) is far above H₂S (−60 °C), H₂Se (−41 °C), H₂Te (−2 °C) — hydrogen bonding needs extra energy to break.
  • Without H-bonding, water would be a gas at room temperature (compare CH₄, 10 electrons, bp −162 °C).
  • Ice is less dense than liquid water: in ice, H-bonds hold molecules in an open hexagonal lattice with fixed, relatively long separations.
  • On melting, the lattice partially collapses and molecules pack closer together — density rises to a maximum at 4 °C.
  • Consequence: ice floats, insulating water below — aquatic life survives winters.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The density explanation must include the open lattice / molecules held further apart idea — "ice is lighter" alone scores nothing. When comparing H₂O with H₂S, state that H₂S has no hydrogen bonding (S is not F/O/N) even though it has more electrons.

Spec 7.4 Predicting H-Bonds

6. Predicting Hydrogen Bonding in Any Molecule

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Check the structure: is there an H atom directly bonded to F, O or N? If yes → can H-bond with itself (CH₃OH, C₂H₅NH₂, CH₃COOH: yes).
  • Ethanal (CH₃CHO) cannot H-bond with itself — none of its H atoms are bonded to O (they are on C).
  • Dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃) cannot H-bond with itself (no O–H), but its lone pairs can H-bond with water.
  • Distinguish "H-bonds with itself" from "H-bonds with water" — examiners ask both.
  • Apply to analogues: H₂S, HCl, CH₄ → no H-bonding; HF, NH₃, H₂O, alcohols, amines, carboxylic acids → yes.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Mark schemes quote: "none of the hydrogen atoms in ethanal are bonded to an oxygen atom (or another highly electronegative atom)" — learn that reasoning, not just the verdict. A molecule with only lone pairs (ethers, aldehydes, ketones) can accept H-bonds from water even though it cannot donate them to itself.

Spec 7.5 IMF & Physical Properties

7. Boiling Points, Volatility & the Four Key Trends

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Alkanes ↑ chain length: CH₄ (−162) → C₈H₁₈ (126 °C) — more electrons ⇒ stronger London forces ⇒ higher boiling point.
  • Branching ↓ boiling point: pentane 36 > 2-methylbutane 28 > 2,2-dimethylpropane 9.5 °C — branching makes molecules compact, reducing surface area for contact.
  • Alcohols vs alkanes: ethanol (78 °C) vs propane (−42 °C) — both have 26 electrons, but ethanol's O–H gives hydrogen bonding ⇒ low volatility.
  • HF to HI: HF (19.5 °C) is anomalously high (H-bonding); HCl (−85) → HBr (−67) → HI (−35) rise as electrons increase.
  • Boiling overcomes intermolecular forces only — covalent bonds within molecules stay intact.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The deadliest error in the paper: "boiling breaks the covalent bonds". It does not — only the IMFs. For branching, say "smaller surface area for contact ⇒ weaker London forces", not "fewer electrons" (isomers have identical electron counts). Volatility is the mirror image: stronger IMF ⇒ lower volatility.

Spec 7.6 Solvents & Solubility

8. Choosing a Solvent — Like Dissolves Like

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Ionic compounds in water: polar water molecules surround the ions (hydration, ion–dipole attraction) and pull them into solution.
  • Simple alcohols in water: the O–H group H-bonds with water ⇒ methanol/ethanol are fully miscible; long carbon chains reduce solubility.
  • Halogenoalkanes + water: immiscible — despite a polar C–X bond, they cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, and water's own H-bonded network resists them.
  • Non-aqueous solvents: non-polar solutes (I₂, oils, grease) dissolve in non-polar solvents (cyclohexane) via London forces — "like dissolves like".
  • The iodine partition: I₂ in water = pale yellow/brown; I₂ in cyclohexane = purple upper layer.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

For "why doesn't bromoethane mix with water?" the mark point is inability to form hydrogen bonds with water — "it is polar" is not enough and can contradict the mark scheme. For ionic solids, name the ion–dipole attraction / hydration of ions. Linking lattice energy vs hydration enthalpy is Topic 12 extension — label it if used.

Covalent O–H bond (reference — NOT an IMF)464 kJ mol⁻¹
Hydrogen bond (strongest IMF)≈ 20–40 kJ mol⁻¹
Permanent dipole–permanent dipole≈ 5 kJ mol⁻¹
London forces (I₂, 106 electrons)≈ 10–60 kJ mol⁻¹
London forces (CH₄, 10 electrons)≈ 2 kJ mol⁻¹

Engine A · Syllabus 7.5

Intermolecular Forces & Boiling Points

5 concept cards
1. Alkane chain length (7.5i)
CH₄ −162 · C₂H₆ −89 · C₃H₈ −42 · C₄H₁₀ −0.5 · C₅H₁₂ 36 · C₆H₁₄ 69 · C₈H₁₈ 126 °C

Explanation chain: longer chain ⇒ more electrons ⇒ larger, more easily distorted electron clouds ⇒ stronger London forces ⇒ more energy needed to separate the molecules ⇒ higher boiling point.

Longer chains also have greater surface area for contact between neighbouring molecules — the two factors reinforce each other.

Learning goals
  • Quote the alkane boiling-point trend with at least three data points.
  • Write the full causal chain: electrons → London forces → energy → boiling point.
Examiner tips
  • "Bigger molecules have stronger bonds" is wrong on two counts — London forces are not bonds, and size alone is not the reason (say electrons).
  • Alkanes have ONLY London forces — a clean system for isolating the electron-count effect.
2. Branching — the isomer test (7.5ii)
C₅H₁₂ isomers: pentane 36 · 2-methylbutane 28 · 2,2-dimethylpropane 9.5 °C

All three isomers have identical molecular formulas and identical electron counts (42 e⁻) — so electron count cannot explain the difference.

Explanation: branching makes the molecule more spherical/compact ⇒ smaller surface area for contact between molecules ⇒ weaker London forces ⇒ lower boiling point. The most branched isomer boils lowest.

Learning goals
  • Explain the isomer trend using surface area, explicitly noting electron counts are equal.
  • Predict the boiling-point order of any set of alkane isomers from structure.
Examiner tips
  • The phrase "surface area for contact" (or "points of contact") is the mark point — "smaller molecule" is too vague.
  • Isomer questions exist precisely to catch students who only memorised "more electrons".
3. Alcohols vs alkanes — same electrons, different world (7.5iii)
C₂H₅OH 78 °C  vs  C₃H₈ −42 °C — both have 26 electrons

Explanation: with equal electron counts, London forces are similar. The difference is ethanol's polar O–H group ⇒ hydrogen bonding between molecules — far stronger than London forces alone ⇒ much more energy needed ⇒ higher boiling point and low volatility.

This is why ethanol is a liquid fuel that evaporates slowly, while propane (a similar-mass alkane) is sold under pressure as a gas.

Learning goals
  • Use the 26-electron pair as the model "controlled experiment" for proving H-bonding matters.
  • Define volatility and link it to IMF strength.
Examiner tips
  • Always state the control: "similar number of electrons / similar relative molecular mass, so London forces are comparable" — then name H-bonding as the extra force.
  • Ethanal (CH₃CHO, bp 20 °C) sits in between: polar (dipole–dipole) but no H-bonding to itself — a favourite comparison.
4. Hydrogen halides — the HF anomaly (7.5iv)
HF 19.5 · HCl −85 · HBr −67 · HI −35 °C

HF: anomalously high — hydrogen bonding (H directly on F, the most electronegative element).

HCl → HBr → HI: no hydrogen bonding (Cl, Br, I are too large / insufficiently electronegative); boiling points rise because electron count increases (18 → 36 → 54) ⇒ stronger London forces.

The same pattern appears in Group 6 hydrides: H₂O 100 °C vs H₂S −60, H₂Se −41, H₂Te −2 °C.

Learning goals
  • Explain both limbs of the trend: the HF anomaly and the HCl→HI rise.
  • Transfer the pattern to H₂O / H₂S / H₂Se / H₂Te.
Examiner tips
  • Do not claim HCl hydrogen-bonds — only F, O and N qualify.
  • Graph questions: describe the anomaly first, then the trend, each with its own mechanism. Two mechanisms, two mark clusters.
5. Volatility — boiling's mirror image

Volatility = how readily a liquid evaporates at a given temperature. It is the inverse of boiling point: stronger IMFs ⇒ lower volatility.

Order of volatility (most → least): propane (London, weak) > ethanal (dipole–dipole) > ethanol (H-bonding). Perfumes and fuels are designed around exactly this principle.

The unbreakable rule: phase changes (melting, boiling, evaporating) overcome intermolecular forces only. The covalent bonds inside each molecule survive — steam still contains H₂O molecules, not H and O atoms.

Learning goals
  • Rank volatility from structure and justify with IMF type.
  • State unambiguously what boiling does and does not break.
Examiner tips
  • "Boiling breaks covalent bonds" is the single most penalised sentence in this topic. Write "intermolecular forces" every time.
  • For ionic compounds, melting overcomes ionic bonds (that is allowed — ionic bonding is the attraction between ions, not molecules).

Steam contains intact H₂O molecules. This sentence alone can sink a 4-mark explanation.

High volatility means easy evaporation — weak IMFs, low boiling point. Read the question's direction carefully.

Engine B · Syllabus 7.2–7.4

Hydrogen Bonding in Water & Beyond

5 concept cards
1. The two requirements for hydrogen bonding
(1) H covalently bonded to F, O or N  AND  (2) a lone pair on F/O/N of a neighbour

Why F/O/N: they are small and highly electronegative ⇒ a very polar bond ⇒ H carries a strong $\delta^+$ and can approach the neighbour's lone pair closely.

Geometry: the strongest interaction is linear — donor–H ⋯ lone pair at ~180°.

Learning goals
  • State both requirements — examiners mark them separately.
  • Explain why Cl and S fail the test despite being electronegative.
Examiner tips
  • "H bonded to an electronegative atom" without naming F/O/N loses a mark — HCl is electronegative but does not qualify.
  • The dotted line in diagrams must connect H to the lone pair, not to the atom's centre.
2. Water — up to 4 hydrogen bonds per molecule (7.2)

Structure: the O atom has 2 bonding pairs (O–H) and 2 lone pairs. Each H(δ+) can donate one H-bond; each lone pair can accept one.

Result: each water molecule can engage in up to 4 hydrogen bonds, creating an extensive, constantly rearranging 3-D network in liquid water and a fixed lattice in ice.

This is why water's properties are so extreme for a 10-electron molecule: bp 100 °C, high enthalpy of vaporisation, high surface tension.

Learning goals
  • Derive the "4 H-bonds" figure from 2 H atoms + 2 lone pairs.
  • Link the network to water's high boiling point and enthalpy of vaporisation (Practical ii).
Examiner tips
  • "Water forms 4 hydrogen bonds" needs the justification (2 H + 2 lone pairs) for full credit.
  • Compare with CH₄ (10 e⁻, bp −162 °C) to show the scale of the H-bonding effect.
3. Ammonia and hydrogen fluoride (7.2)

NH₃: 3 H atoms on N but only 1 lone pair — H-bonding is limited by the lone-pair count; bp −33 °C (still anomalously high vs PH₃, −88 °C).

HF: 1 H atom but 3 lone pairs on F — limited by the H count; bp 19.5 °C, anomalously high vs HCl (−85 °C).

Water wins: 2 H + 2 lone pairs = perfectly balanced for 4 bonds per molecule — the most extensive H-bond network of the three.

Learning goals
  • Count H atoms and lone pairs for NH₃, HF and H₂O and predict relative H-bonding extent.
  • Explain why water boils higher than HF despite F being more electronegative than O.
Examiner tips
  • The "2 + 2" argument for water is a classic 2-mark answer — H atoms AND lone pairs both matter.
  • Don't confuse H-bond strength (HF's individual bonds are strongest) with number of bonds (water forms more).
4. Water's anomalous properties (7.3)

Anomaly 1 — boiling/melting point: Group 6 hydrides: H₂S −60, H₂Se −41, H₂Te −2 °C (rising with electrons) — but H₂O is 100 °C, far off the trend line, because of H-bonding.

Anomaly 2 — density of ice: in ice, every molecule is locked into 4 hydrogen bonds in an open hexagonal lattice. Molecules are held further apart than in the liquid ⇒ ice is ~9% less dense ⇒ it floats.

Consequences: lakes freeze from the top down; the ice layer insulates liquid water below; aquatic ecosystems survive winter. See the ❄️ toggle in the Dipole Lab.

Learning goals
  • Explain both anomalies with the H-bonding mechanism.
  • Use "open lattice / molecules held further apart" for the density answer.
Examiner tips
  • For H₂O vs H₂S comparisons, state explicitly that H₂S has NO hydrogen bonding — more electrons cannot compensate.
  • "Ice is lighter than water" is not an explanation — density = mass/volume; the volume is what changes.

Engine C · Syllabus 7.6

Solubility & Solvent Choice

5 concept cards
1. Like dissolves like
Solute dissolves when solute–solvent attractions are comparable to (or stronger than) the attractions each has to itself

Polar/ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents (water). Non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents (cyclohexane, hexane).

Why mixing can fail: water molecules H-bond strongly to each other. A solute that cannot offer comparable attractions (H-bonds or ion–dipole) is "squeezed out" — the water network prefers itself.

Learning goals
  • Predict miscibility from the IMF match between solute and solvent.
  • Explain immiscibility in terms of the solvent's own strong attractions.
Examiner tips
  • "Like dissolves like" is a rule of thumb, not an explanation — always name the actual forces involved.
  • For water, the phrase "water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other" is usually a required mark point.
2. Ionic compounds in water (7.6i)

Mechanism — hydration: water is polar (O δ−, H δ+). The O(δ−) ends surround cations; the H(δ+) ends surround anions. These ion–dipole attractions are called hydration.

Energy balance: hydration releases energy (exothermic $\Delta_\text{hyd}H$ — Topic 12 link). If hydration compensates for breaking the lattice, the solid dissolves: NaCl ✓, CaCO₃ ✗.

In non-polar solvents: cyclohexane has no dipole ⇒ no ion–dipole attraction ⇒ ionic solids are insoluble.

Learning goals
  • Describe hydration shells with correct dipole orientations (O toward cations, H toward anions).
  • Name the ion–dipole attraction explicitly.
Examiner tips
  • Diagram questions: water molecules must be drawn with O pointing at the positive ion — orientation is marked.
  • Dissolving is a physical change — the ions separate; no bonds within water are broken.
3. Simple alcohols in water (7.6ii)

Methanol and ethanol are fully miscible: their O–H groups hydrogen-bond with water — solute–solvent attractions match water–water attractions.

Chain-length effect: as the non-polar carbon chain grows (butanol, pentanol…), the hydrocarbon "tail" disrupts water's H-bond network without compensating attractions ⇒ solubility falls sharply. Long-chain alcohols are effectively insoluble.

Learning goals
  • Explain miscibility of small alcohols via H-bonding with water.
  • Explain the solubility drop with chain length (non-polar tail dominates).
Examiner tips
  • "Alcohols dissolve because they are polar" is half-credit at best — name hydrogen bonding with water.
  • The two-part molecule (polar head, non-polar tail) is the key idea for soaps and detergents later.
4. Halogenoalkanes and water (7.6iii)

Observation: bromoethane and water form two layers — immiscible.

Explanation (the syllabus phrasing): although the C–X bond is polar, halogenoalkanes cannot form hydrogen bonds with water. Water molecules H-bond strongly to each other and are not disrupted by the weaker dipole interactions the halogenoalkane can offer ⇒ the liquids separate.

With non-polar solvents: halogenoalkanes mix freely with cyclohexane (London forces and dipole–induced dipole interactions).

Learning goals
  • Explain immiscibility using "cannot form hydrogen bonds with water".
  • Contrast with behaviour in non-polar solvents.
Examiner tips
  • Saying "bromoethane is non-polar" is wrong — it IS polar; the mark point is specifically about H-bonding.
  • Layer order: most halogenoalkanes are denser than water ⇒ organic layer below (check data in the question).
5. Non-aqueous solvents (7.6iv)

Principle: choose a solvent whose IMFs match the solute's. Non-polar solutes (iodine, oils, grease, waxes) dissolve in non-polar solvents via London forces.

The iodine partition (Practical i): shake I₂ with water + cyclohexane. I₂ is non-polar ⇒ prefers cyclohexane ⇒ purple upper layer; the aqueous layer stays pale yellow/brown. (In water, I₂ gives only a weak induced interaction; the brown colour comes from a faint charge-transfer complex — the purple is the exam-recognised non-polar colour.)

Applications: dry-cleaning (non-polar solvents remove grease from fabric), extracting organic products from reaction mixtures, decaffeination.

Learning goals
  • Describe the iodine partition observation and explain it with London forces.
  • Justify solvent choice for a given solute in an unfamiliar context.
Examiner tips
  • Iodine colours are a recall mark: purple in non-polar solvents, yellow/brown in water.
  • "Cyclohexane dissolves iodine because they react" — no reaction occurs; it is purely physical mixing.

⚡ Solubility trap pairs
❌ Trap: "bromoethane is non-polar" ✅ Truth: polar — but cannot H-bond with water

The C–Br dipole is real; it just cannot compete with water's H-bond network. Precision matters here.

❌ Trap: "like dissolves like" as a full answer ✅ Truth: name the actual forces

The rule organises your thinking; the marks go to ion–dipole / H-bonding / London forces named explicitly.

❌ Trap: hydration shells drawn backwards ✅ Truth: O(δ−) → cation; H(δ+) → anion

Opposite charges attract — the oxygen end of water always faces the positive ion in marked diagrams.

❌ Trap: iodine + cyclohexane = a reaction ✅ Truth: physical dissolution via London forces

No chemical change — I₂ molecules simply mix with cyclohexane molecules. The purple colour is molecular I₂.

❌ Trap: "ethanol is polar so it dissolves" ✅ Truth: it H-bonds with water

Polarity alone is the bromoethane trap. For alcohols, the O–H group's hydrogen bonding is the mark point.

❌ Trap: all alcohols are miscible ✅ Truth: the carbon chain fights the O–H group

Methanol/ethanol/propan-1-ol: miscible. Butanol: 8 g per 100 cm³. Octanol: effectively insoluble.

📝 Examiner Report & Misconceptions — Intermolecular Forces

H-bonding conditions

H-bond needs H bonded to N, O or F AND a lone pair on N/O/F of another molecule. NH₃ self H-bonds; HBr/H₂S/SiH₄ do NOT.

London forces driver

More electrons → stronger London/dispersion forces. HBr (most electrons) has the strongest dispersion, not the most polar.

QWC — compare strengths

Must COMPARE relative strengths, not just say “weak” (London) or “strong” (H-bond). Number of H-bonds per molecule matters.

H₂O anomaly

H₂O has a much higher bp than expected — H-bonding effect larger than the London trend. Graph point P is the outlier.

Ion–dipole vs dipole–dipole

Hydration = ion–permanent dipole attraction (O δ− toward Na⁺). Not ion–ion (NaCl is dissociated).

VdW only

SiH₄ has only London forces (no permanent dipole); NH₃/HBr/H₂S have permanent dipoles too.
